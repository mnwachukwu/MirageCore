# Changes from the VB6 original

What this rewrite does differently from Mirage Online 3.0.3 — additions, rebalances,
fixes carried across, and what is still missing.

## Seamless scrolling world

VB6 showed one 16×12 map at a time, with a hard cut and a brief reload at every border. The C# rewrite renders the player's map **and its eight neighbors** as one continuous world: the camera follows the player and clamps only when the grid runs out of maps, so crossing a border is just another step — no flash, no input pause.

- **Pre-loaded neighbors.** On entering a map the server names the eight surrounding maps; the client serves unchanged ones from a persistent disk cache and downloads only what it lacks, so repeated crossings cost almost no bandwidth.
- **One live world.** Players, NPCs, and items on every visible map are fully synced and interactive — you watch fights unfold across a border and pick up an item lying just across the seam.
- **Combat across borders.** Melee and spells reach across map edges using world-space range and adjacency. Safe-zone protection applies when *either* side of the border is safe.
- **Targeting that follows.** A target lock is tracked by identity, not map slot, so it stays on the same creature across a border and clears only when that creature dies or gives up. Range is measured from the centered viewport, so clamping the camera never widens it.
- **Cross-map pursuit.** Hostile NPCs chase a fleeing player up to one map beyond their spawn — attacking across the seam or stepping through it — but never into a safe zone, and they return home once the target is lost.
- **Bounded cost.** Work scales with what is visible, not with how many maps are loaded: rendering iterates only the on-screen rectangle, broadcasts target only players who can see a map, and NPC AI runs only on observed maps.

## Improvements and additions

#### Layered world — bridges & a second walkable layer (new)

A genuine **second traversable plane** — a **fringe layer** — lets a player or NPC stand *over* or *under* the same tile: the deck of a **bridge** and the ground beneath it are both walkable, **including across seamless map seams**. Layer is a first-class coordinate consulted by movement, collision, rendering, lighting, blood, particles, items, corpses, combat, line-of-sight, and territory capture. VB6 had a single walkable ground; its extra tile layers were décor painted over that one plane.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| Two walkable planes + ramps | **Ground** and **Fringe** are two full walkable planes at every tile, each shaped by its own attributes (a `Blocked` attribute is a wall for walkers on *that* plane only). A **Layer Ramp** is the sole connector: mount it from its ground-side foot and it carries you onto the deck. A ramp is **solid from below** and **depth-gated** — a big NPC needs a ramp deep enough for its whole footprint. Your layer is **sticky** (it flips only by crossing a ramp) and **persists across seams** and through warps that target the deck. NPC pathfinding routes across ramps and seams layer-correctly, and the client predicts the layer so movement never rubber-bands. |
| Over/under rendering + the "lift" dim | The world draws in six passes — ground tiles, ground entities, deck tiles, deck entities, a topmost **Canopy** art stack, then names and bars — so a walker on a bridge draws above the deck and above anyone beneath it, while canopy décor (treetops, roofs) draws over *everyone*. Standing on the deck dims the whole ground level so it reads as below you; overhead names, bars, and target arrows always float on top. |
| Two-plane lighting (occlusion) | At night each plane carries its own light map, composited as light **occlusion**: a ground torch's glow spills up through open or grated deck but is blocked by solid deck, so from a bridge you see light leaking around its edges and, through a grate, the torches below — even while the ground level is dimmed. A light belongs to its emitter's plane. On flat or daylit maps the split collapses back to single-pass lighting. |
| Layer-aware combat & line of sight | Melee and spells **connect within a plane**, with one bridge between them: a person **on a ramp** links both planes, so a ground attacker at a ramp's foot can strike a target on the ramp and be struck back, but a plain ground point and a plain deck point never connect. Line-of-sight obstacles are read on the **shooter's plane** — a closed fringe railing blocks a fringe-to-fringe shot but not a ground shot passing beneath it, and a ramp reads solid to a ground shot. Acquisition still ranges across planes so you can see and cycle a target above or below you; the shot won't *connect* until you're layer-legal (the target arrow grays). Blood, dropped items, and corpses stay on the plane they landed on, and king-of-the-hill capture credit is per-plane. |
| Per-plane doors, warps & items | A **warp**, a **Key door**, a **KeyOpen** trigger, or a **tile-spawned item** can be authored on either plane and fires only for a walker on that plane — so a bridge deck and the ground under it can carry independent warps or doors. A door tracks open/closed state per plane, and a warp can deliberately deliver you onto the deck. |
| Editor authoring | A **Ground / Fringe** selector governs which plane attribute and light edits land on; a third **Canopy** tile-art stack paints the over-everything décor; the **Layer Ramp** tool stamps a ramp with an arrow pointing the way it lifts (amber when a contiguous ramp block mixes directions, a red frame when a block has no valid ground mount). Lights are placed per tile *and* plane. The hover readout shows the active plane and stack, **Ctrl+Alt+scroll** switches plane, and the layer being edited renders at full strength while the others dim. |

#### Guild, war & territory system (new)

VB6 defined guild data structures but never implemented the behavior. The C# rewrite builds the whole thing fresh.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| Guilds & social | Per-account guilds (create/disband/invite/apply/promote/demote/leadership-transfer), a tabbed **Social (G)** panel (Friends / Ignore / Guild), per-account friends and ignore lists, guild and officer chat (`/g`, `/o`), an overhead guild name in a leader-chosen color, and open-for-membership discovery with offline-safe applications delivered by mail. |
| Guildmate friendly fire | Guild members cannot damage each other — melee or harmful spells — mirroring party protection, so a guild fights as a unit. The lone exception is an **arena** map, where guild protection lifts so guildmates can spar; party protection has no such exception. Enemy-guild and guild-war combat is unaffected, being always between different guilds. |
| Levels, perks, tax & quests | Guild XP from mob kills plus **guild quests** drives levels 0-5, each unlocking a cumulative perk (drop rate, wear/reagent skip, +EXP, double-drop, mob-kill vault gold). A weekly tax on the founding weekday keeps perks on, settled at a daily 00:00 "debts before credits" pass. A shared vault holds gold and **valor** (war currency), with a weekly financial-health dashboard. |
| Grudge wars | Leader-confirmed war declarations with a cost and daily-maintenance economy; **durability-only** war deaths (the vault absorbs 75% of doubled wear as a sink, no item or EXP loss), an attrition tug-of-war, per-target diminishing returns, bankruptcy auto-loss, consensual wagers, and peace/cold-war end states. |
| Territory control | Map-groups can be flagged **territories** owned by a guild; all-comers PvE kills there feed the owner's vault (weeks-held multiplier). A weekly **war night** runs a live king-of-the-hill contest — 2-5 capture points, setup radius-walls and NPC despawn, 20 minutes of scoring with a defender edge — whose winner takes the territory. Participants fight under war-death rules in-zone; per-viewer flags, circles, names, and an in-world capture/score HUD show the contest. |
| Seasonal leaderboard | A 13-week season scores guilds by weeks-in-control (plus a consecutive-hold bonus) then territory-war K/D, with vault and **active-member (mailed, claimable)** placing payouts, a perpetual archive, and a standings tab. Overhead prestige flair: a leader-toggled rank word, and crossed swords over guilds you're warring. |
#### Player mail system (new)

VB6 had no player mail — only a global MOTD. The engine-to-player mail primitive that shipped with the guild layer is here grown into a full player-to-player system; the marketplace builds on the same delayed-delivery stack, while direct trade is its instant, mail-free counterpart.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| Player-to-player mail | A per-account **mailbox** (the **Mail (M)** panel) with a sortable, resizable inbox table (From / Date / Subject), a reading pane, and a compose view addressed by **account name** — so one letter reaches the recipient on whichever character they play. Delivery works online (pushed live) or offline (persisted for next login), bounded to the 50 most-recent messages. A message can carry **attachments** — up to 4 stacks of gold and/or items — **escrowed off the sender at send** so the goods leave their bag immediately, and refunded if the recipient account doesn't exist. The recipient **claims** attachments into their inventory: currency always fits and stacks, an item a full bag can't hold is left claimable rather than eaten, and a claimed item keeps the sender's durability. Gold rides as an ordinary currency attachment. **Reply** prefills the sender and prefixes the subject with `RE:`. |
| Delayed delivery, in transit & outbox | Player-origin mail takes a **random 10-15 minutes** to mature, so goods and gold move with a deliberate lag; engine mail (guild notices, season payouts) stays instant. Until it matures a message rides **in transit** — a grayed row with a note on **both ends**, whose attachments can't be claimed and which can't be deleted. Composing also files the letter in the sender's **Outbox**, an independent copy showing the same progression, so both parties watch one delivery clock; deleting an outbox entry never touches the recipient's copy. A periodic sweep flips matured mail to delivered and re-syncs both owners, and "in transit" renders against the server's clock, not the player's. The maturity gate is server-authoritative — a modified client can neither claim nor delete early. |
| Ignore-filtered inbox | A letter from someone on your per-account **ignore list** is still delivered and stored server-side, but your client **hides it** from the inbox and the unread badge — silencing their mail with no server block. Un-ignoring makes the message and any unclaimed attachments reappear, so the filter is fully reversible and nothing is destroyed. Matching is by account login, case-insensitive. Your own outbox and engine mail are never filtered. |

#### Player marketplace (new)

The mail system doubles as the delivery backbone for a **marketplace** — an asynchronous auction house where goods and gold move as delayed mail. VB6 had no player economy beyond fixed-price NPC shops.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| Marketplace listings | A player-run marketplace, opened from a third button on the **Inn panel** (browsable at any inn) and movement-locking like the bank. A seller lists an inventory item at a fixed gold price: the item is **escrowed off them immediately**, capped at a handful of active listings per seller, and **untradeable** items (gold, the casting reagent, war valor, anything else flagged) can't be listed. Listings are global, persisted, and survive restarts and an offline seller. **Browse** shows everyone's listings (item / seller / price, sortable) with hover tooltips; **My Listings** shows your own, each cancelable to reclaim the escrowed item to your bag, or your mailbox if it's full. |
| Buying, delivery & the sale tax | Buying charges the listing price and hands the goods to the same **delayed-mail** path as personal mail — item to the buyer's mailbox, payout to the seller's, each riding the 10-15 minute delay before it can be claimed, and the seller can be offline throughout. A **sale tax** (a gold sink) is withheld from the payout and shown to the seller **up front** at listing time: price, tax, and exact net before committing. The whole buy is server-authoritative — price, ownership, funds, and standing at an inn are all re-checked. Tax rate and per-seller cap are tunable. |
| Listing lifetime, partial buys & sales history | Listings **expire after 30 days**, at which point a background sweep mails the item back to the seller, so an untouched listing never strands goods. A **currency** listing is priced **per unit** and can be bought in part — the buyer names how many units and pays exactly that many times the unit price (no rounding), and the listing shrinks rather than clearing. Every completed sale is written to a rolling **sales history**, surfaced in the panel's Sales tab (item / buyer / net / date) and kept on disk as an audit log for operators. |
| Live board, partial listing & refresh | The board is **live**: when anyone lists, buys, cancels, or a listing expires, every open browser updates on the spot. A **Refresh** button re-pulls on demand as a fallback. A currency stack can also be **listed** in part — the seller chooses how many units to put up at the per-unit price and keeps the rest, mirroring the partial buy. |

#### Direct trade (new)

Where the marketplace moves goods asynchronously, **direct trade** is its real-time counterpart: two players standing together swap items and gold in a shared window, with no delivery lag and no mailbox. VB6 had no player-to-player exchange at all — its `/trade` opened the NPC shop, since renamed to `/shop` here.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| Two-party trade window | A live trade window started either by **`/trade <name>`** or the **Trade** item on a player's right-click menu — identical whether you right-click a name in chat or a character in the world. The target gets an accept/decline prompt; if both players invite each other, the second invite counts as an acceptance. The window shows both sides' staged offers and each party's confirm state, updated live. |
| Proximity, movement lock & auto-cancel | Trading requires both parties within **casting range** (a world-space radius of 5 tiles, the same circle spells use), measured across seamless borders rather than by map number, so neighbors near a shared edge can trade. The open window **locks movement** for both. If they're separated anyway — a death and respawn, a warp — a server sweep notices and **auto-cancels**, returning everything. |
| Escrow, mutual confirm & atomic swap | An offered item is **escrowed off the bag the moment it's staged**; currency is offered by amount, and **untradeable** items can't be offered at all. Any change to either offer **clears both confirmations**, so no one confirms against an offer that then changes. The exchange fires only when both confirm and is **atomic** — the server pre-checks that each side has room, so a full bag can never leave a trade half-completed. Canceling, disconnecting, or a proximity break returns both players' staged goods (bag, or mailbox if full). Staged goods are **persisted with the character**, so an escrowed item survives a crash mid-trade and returns on next login, and the swap itself commits through a write-ahead journal, so a crash at the instant of exchange can never duplicate or lose an item. An interrupted trade never eats items. |

#### World records & world settings (new)

VB6's world was a fixed shape compiled into both halves of the game: 255 of each record family, every map exactly 16×12, and nothing about a world recorded anywhere except the records themselves. Here a world folder describes itself, and both the server and the editor size themselves to what it says.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| `world.json` | A world folder's root file states three things about itself: its **name**, the **size new maps are created at**, and its **record ceilings**. Optional — a folder without one runs on the stock answers. The name is for whoever is *holding* the world: the editor's title bar and recent-worlds list, the server window, the logs. **It never reaches a player**, who sees the game's name instead; the two exist so an operator can tell a live world from a test copy of it, and that is the only job it has. Edited under **World → World Settings**. |
| Configurable record ceilings | Items, NPCs, shops, spells, quests, conversations, maps and map-groups each carry their own ceiling, defaulting to **1000** where VB6 had a hard-compiled 255. Nothing on the wire constrains them — the protocol is JSON, so a record number is a number — but a client compiled against one ceiling would reject a record past it, so the server **states its ceilings in a pre-login hello** and the client sizes to match. Raising one costs three known local things: the server pads that family in its data folder, allocates one array for it, and the editor's pickers list that many rows. Per-character shapes — inventory, bank, hotkeys, spellbook, character slots — stay compile-time constants, because changing one of those is a save-format migration rather than a setting. |
| Per-map dimensions | A map is **16×12 unless it says otherwise**, and any map can be resized in its properties; `world.json` sets only what a *new* map starts at. A map's size is the shape of its tile array and nothing else records it, so a cached map carries its own size structurally. **Maps joined by an edge must all be the same size** — world coordinates run continuously across a seam, so a mismatch would make a step across one land somewhere other than where it looks — and the editor refuses to resize a linked map rather than allowing it. Drawing costs the same at every size, since the client only ever draws what fits on screen; what grows is loading a map and searching one. Past **128 tiles on an axis** the editor warns, and the format stops at 65,535, which is how wide a warp's destination coordinate is. |
| Resizing is itemized and final | **Resizing cannot be undone.** Shrinking discards everything outside the new bounds and nothing writes it anywhere first, so before committing, the editor counts exactly what would go — authored tiles, placed lights, NPC spawn pins, and warps pointing in *from other maps* — and says to copy the folder first. Only *authored* tiles count, so trimming blank margin reads as free. |
| Map groups | A map can name a **group**, and the group supplies any property the map leaves unset: music, moral, indoors, the two lighting overrides, the boot point, and the map-enter/leave greeting. **The map's own value always wins**, so a group is a default rather than an override — a multi-map building sets its greeting or its moral once. A group also carries a name and display name, which slot into the map-name chain between a map's display name and its raw name. A group flagged a **territory** is the contestable unit the guild war system fights over. |
| Boot point (Respawn) | A map — or its group — can name where a player who leaves it other than by walking ends up. It applies **on death and on logout alike**, and it **outranks the player's own Inn-purchased spawn point**: what dying in a place costs is the map author's call, not something a player can buy their way out of. VB6 had a single global respawn. |
| Checking a world | **World → Check World** reads every record against every other and reports what does not agree. **Between maps:** a link joining two sizes, answering only one way, or naming a map that is not there; a warp or boot point naming a tile its destination has not got; a spawn pin or placed light outside the map holding it; a group index no group backs. **Between records:** anything naming an NPC, item, spell, quest or class that is not there — where "not there" covers a number out of range *and* a slot nobody has authored, which is the common one, since renumbering content leaves references pointing at blanks. **Reachability:** a shop with no keeper (a shop is opened by talking to its keeper and by nothing else); a conversation offering to open a shop or a quest menu its speaker does not hold; a quest whose prerequisite chain loops back on itself. Each finding names the record and tile it is on and clicks through to it. Nothing is changed — the sweep only reads. VB6 validated no authored content at all, and had no way to; the checks that exist here are only possible because a map states its own size and a world states its own ceilings, so there is something to check *against*. |
| Map greetings | A map or group can carry a line spoken on entering it and another on leaving, attributed to a named speaker. Each field inherits from the group independently, so a building can define one greeting for all its rooms. |

#### Running a server (new)

VB6 shipped one executable with its limits compiled in and its administration done by hand-editing files. The server here is configured, measured, and moderated while it runs.

| Addition | What it does |
|---|---|
| Configurable player slots + load benchmark | How many players a server accepts is a setting, defaulting to a deliberately small **20** against a protocol ceiling of 500. The right number depends on the machine, so the server window **measures it** with a load benchmark rather than asking an operator to guess. Every server-side scan walks the configured count rather than the ceiling, so a small world neither does the work nor holds the arrays for slots it will never use. A configurable number of slots is **reserved for moderators and above**, so a full server can still be moderated. |
| Login queue | A server at capacity **queues** rather than refusing. The line is reconsidered twice a second, off the game thread, and a waiting player is told their position. |
| Moderation while running | Bans, kicks and mutes are applied from the server window or the client's moderation panel, all keyed to the **account** rather than the character. Kicks and mutes take an optional duration in minutes. Every penalty can be **lifted** — VB6 could apply one and had no way to take it back short of hand-editing files. A separate **machine ban** (`/hwban`) answers the case an account ban cannot: a banned player registering again. |
| God mode | An admin toggle that makes a character untouchable by damage, for walking through content that would otherwise kill the tester. |
| Concurrent editor sessions | Several editors can be connected to one live server. A record is **locked when an editor dirties it** and released when it saves or discards; reading takes nothing, so the table only ever names people who actually have changes in hand. A session that drops takes its locks with it, so a crashed editor cannot wedge a record shut. |
| Remembered servers | The client and editor keep a list of servers they have connected to, each with its pinned certificate, so returning to one is a click and a changed certificate is noticed. Traffic is encrypted in transit. |

#### Balance Changes

The original Mirage Source 3.0.3 was horribly unbalanced. These are general tweaks and improvements to bring balance to the simplistic duality the original worked within. Formulas were checked against a standalone simulator, `CombatSim`. It is one of the balance simulators, which are the part of the tools repository that is **not** published (see the main README) — it has no dependency on the engine, so it is kept out of the game's solution and release cycle. The specific magnitudes are playtest-calibrated starting points, and tuning constants are isolated for fast retuning.

| Change | What's different |
|---|---|
| Combat rebalance (symmetric mirror) | A ground-up rework toward **all-level PvP balance by symmetry**. **STR and INT are mechanically identical offense stats** — a spell is a weapon swing delivered at range, dealing the same damage per point off INT that a weapon does off STR. **DEF is the single defense axis**, resisting physical and magic identically through one universal mitigation value (magic penetration and the separate magic-mit are gone), and every defensive piece now wears on any hit. **Magic is negated exactly like melee**: a shield blocks it and a shield-free target dodges it, at the same SP cost as their melee counterparts. **HP is a clean single-stat DEF quadratic** — the STR→HP bonus, a warrior-only survivability edge, is deleted on players and NPCs alike. The **only intended asymmetry is RANGE**, governed by **SPD→run speed**: SPD scales how fast you run (diminishing curve, hard-capped, run-only), so a faster mage kites a slower warrior and a faster warrior closes on a fleeing caster; at equal SPD the duel falls back to base balance. Casters keep a mana economy — spells cost MP as the range tax, with the pool sized (~2×) to burst roughly one full fight. NPCs mirror all of this, with ±10% spell-damage variance and the unchanged PvE tankiness bump, and NPC kill-EXP keeps its HP-driven bonus retargeted from STR to **DEF**. |
| Level-based mitigation, damage floor & PvP cut | Makes **DEF a defensive sidegrade rather than a win-button** without leaving low-DEF builds helpless. Player mitigation is **level-primary**: a baseline everyone gets from their level, plus a DEF bonus on top (zero at Def=0) — so a pure offense build is survivable at its level while a DEF build weathers roughly 10× the hits and takes a roughly **even** PvP win rate rather than an outright win. A **universal min-damage floor** means every landed hit deals at least **12%** of its raw through any amount of mitigation, so stacking DEF is never total immunity and a weak-offense build can always chip a tank down. A **player hitting an NPC uses a higher 35% PvE floor**, so a half-offense hybrid whose raw sits under a tanky mob's mitigation kills at ~3× a pure's time instead of ~10×; pures are unaffected, and PvP and NPC→player keep the 12% floor. **Player-vs-player damage is cut to ×0.5**, making PvP markedly more survivable than fighting an on-level NPC and giving DEF's edge room to matter. On the PvE side a flat **−30% NPC-to-player damage** disfavor is kept alongside the up-to-+30% HP favor, so on-level mobs stay impactful without spiking a squishy build down. Every landed hit — player, NPC, melee, spell — resolves through one shared chokepoint so the floor and PvP cut can't drift between paths; MP/SP *drain* spells are resource attrition and take neither. |
| Caster resource model — sustainable damage + a casting reagent | A caster's basic **damage** spell (SubHp) is now stat-sustainable like a warrior's weapon: it costs a trivial fraction of the MP pool, so mana no longer gates a caster's DPS — mana instead pays for **utility** (heals, drains). In its place every SubHp cast consumes a **casting reagent** (reserved **item index 2**), a per-cast upkeep mirroring weapon repair: consumption scales with the spell's power, doubles in the rain exactly as rain doubles weapon wear, and a cast is refused when you're out. NPC casters pay the same trivial pool-fraction cost and consume no reagent, so in-combat regen out-paces the drain and a caster sustains its spell attack much as a player does; a mixed Str/Int mob still weaves melee and magic, and one that does run low on mana drops to melee until it recovers. MP/SP **drains** resolve through the same protection and damage floor as any hit, and SP drains and restores are scaled onto the much smaller SP pool — they move the same *fraction* of SP that the equivalent MP spell moves of MP. |
| Spell & gear gating (INT + class affinity) | Learnable spells are gated by **INT**, not player level — mirroring how a weapon is gated by STR and armor by DEF. A spell's `VitalAmount` is both its power and its INT requirement, so a high-INT caster unlocks a spell exactly as a high-STR warrior wields a heavy weapon. This also aligns players with NPCs, which have always cast, hit, and tanked purely off stats. Each **class carries a head-start on its affinity stats**: VB6's "learn spells `classInt/4` levels early" discount generalizes into an effective-stat bonus applied to STR (weapons), DEF (armor/helmet/shield), and INT (spells) alike, so a high-DEF class equips better armor sooner and a high-INT class learns better spells sooner. It shifts only *when* you can equip or learn, never combat power — two characters at equal stats fight identically regardless of class. The hard class *lock* (an item or spell restricted to one class) is unchanged. Every requirement readout — inventory tooltips, spell tooltips, the shop — shows the effective number with the head-start in parentheses, e.g. `INT 27 (-3)`. VB6 gated spells on level; moving that to INT is a deliberate rebalance deviation. |
| Spell range and targeting symmetry | Spell reach is a Pythagorean circle of radius 5 centered on the caster — the same reach in every direction, cardinal and diagonal. VB6's range was the raw viewport rectangle, asymmetric on both axes, which let mages hit farther along X than Y and forced a bad meta of keeping prey on the long axis; it also needed a separate "mutual range" gate to prevent one-sided cases. The symmetric circle is inherently two-way, so that gate is gone. R=5 is the largest symmetric circle that fits the 16×12 viewport, so the four corner wings stay visible but uncastable. Tab-targeting inherits the same gate — the cycle only lands on entities you could actually cast on. |
| Stamina | Running costs SP and reaching zero forces walking. Blocking and critting both cost SP. Shield-free players can dodge at half the block chance for double the SP cost. All three are impossible at 0 SP. Capped probabilities (all SP gates met): player block/crit/spellcrit 35%, player dodge 15%, NPC block/crit 25%, NPC dodge 10%. Displayed chances in the stats panel are the exact real probabilities; VB6 displayed values twice the actual rate. |
| Combat chance scale dial | A single scale constant controls the granularity of every block/dodge/crit roll. At the live scale of 1 the cap constants read as whole percents (35 / 25 / 15 / 10) and rolls are out of 100. Bump the dial to 10 and the same constants reread as per-mille (3.5% / 2.5% / 1.5% / 1.0%), rolls widen to 1000, and the display formatter divides by 10 so sub-percent probabilities become representable — useful if a playtest pass shows the percent-tier rates fire too often. Formula bodies are unchanged either way; only the roll denominator and display divisor scale. Item drop chances and durability warning procs are unrelated mechanics with deliberate ~20-25% rates and always roll out of 100 regardless of the dial. |
| Combat scaling, damage curves & gear DR | Player and NPC vital pools (HP and MP) use a shifted-quadratic shape so progression isn't flat: the same matchup at level 30 takes meaningfully longer than at level 10. Player HP/MP roughly double at mid-high level and triple at endgame; NPC HP at heavy-tank tiers roughly doubles, while glass-cannon and basic-tier NPCs barely move. Damage — player unarmed melee, player spell power, NPC melee, NPC spell magnitude — uses the same shape with larger offsets, so damage scales *slower* than HP and time-to-kill grows with stat investment rather than staying constant. Low-level numbers stay close to before, so newcomers aren't disadvantaged; the divergence is at the top of the curve. Gear applies diminishing returns rather than adding its full `Power`: a defensive piece's contribution asymptotes at the player's DEF, so over-stacked values give sharply less return than the raw number suggests, while the same item *grows with the player* (a `Power=50` piece gives ~8 mitigation at DEF 10 and ~33 at DEF 100). A weapon's `Power` mirrors two armor pieces' combined mitigation at matched gear, and a spell's `VitalAmount` mirrors a weapon's `Power` exactly — so stats decide matched-gear fights. SP and SP regen stay linear because SP costs are percentages of pool; HP and MP regen scale with their quadratic pools so recovery-as-a-percentage-of-pool stays constant. |
| Player-relative kill EXP | Killing an NPC no longer pays a fixed, mob-intrinsic reward — EXP is computed against the **killing player**, so it scales with how hard *that* fight actually was for *them*. The reward is `scale × expectedTtk × danger`: **expectedTtk** is the mob's HP over the player's normal hit (a mob you out-level and one-shot is worth almost nothing), and **danger** is how big a bite the mob takes out of the player per hit as a fraction of their max HP — a risk premium. `expectedTtk` is computed from stats, **not** the observed swing count, so crits and blocks change how a fight plays out but never the payout: the reward is deterministic per player-and-mob pair. The net is EXP-per-time roughly flat across appropriately-leveled content, rising only for genuinely dangerous fights. That closes farming antipatterns in both directions — trivial low-DEF mobs self-nullify through `expectedTtk`, and risk-free tanks earn no danger premium. A shared kill pays each contributor their own solo rate scaled by their damage share, so a group kill pays less total than a solo. This **replaces** VB6's stat-sum formula, the ±20% trivial/strong level-gap tier, and the flat ExtraHp premium — a boss's inflated HP now lengthens the fight and is paid for automatically. The NPC editor gains a **"for player level" spinner** so designers can scrub a mob's EXP curve across the band they intend to place it in. |
| EXP and progression | EXP is a lifetime total — it never resets on level-up and can decline on death, causing deleveling. Each level-up awards exactly 3 stat points. Kills distribute contribution EXP proportionally by damage dealt; party members on the same map within 5 levels of each other earn stacking rewards for a 1.45× combined payout (see the Party row). Player and NPC toughness use the same shape (STR + DEF + INT at full weight on all three), so a pure mage and a pure warrior with equal stat investment cross the strength-comparison thresholds together — no piecewise formula that would punish hybrid builds. |
| Equipment durability & breakage | Worn gear no longer loses a durability point on every hit. Each hit *rolls* to chip 1 point, with odds that climb as the piece wears: at 75%+ condition a hit chips 25% of the time; 50-74% chips 50%; 25-49% chips 75%; below 25% every hit chips. Healthy gear lasts far longer while a battered piece degrades quickly. When durability reaches 0 the piece **breaks** instead of being destroyed: it drops to 0/max, is automatically unequipped, and stays in the bag, unusable until repaired at a repair shop. The inventory tags it `(Broken)` and the tooltip shows its real durability rather than masking it as full. The same break-not-destroy rule covers the durability damage a **death** inflicts. Death resolves item drops *before* applying that damage, so a piece that breaks is still treated as equipped for the death's drop rules instead of being swept into the always-dropped unequipped bucket — breaking a piece never also forces it to drop. VB6 reduced durability by 1 on every hit and silently deleted the item the instant it hit 0, in combat and on death alike. |
| Death penalty | Equipment takes durability damage, EXP is deducted, and random unequipped items may drop. Players below level 10 are exempt. A large level gap between killer and victim waives penalties for non-PK deaths. PK victims face doubled penalties with no level-gap exemption and lose all unequipped inventory including gold; equipped items have a chance to drop as well. |
| Party | Party members cannot damage each other — melee or harmful spells — regardless of either player's PK status, and parties can form across any PK combination. EXP is awarded as **contribution EXP** (damage share × mob EXP), and an in-band party (partner level gap ≤ 5) earns two stacking rewards on top: a **party bonus** (20% of the contributor's own contribution EXP) and a **partner kill bonus** (25% of the partner's base contribution, paid to every in-band member whose partner contributed — so two players hitting the same mob each get a cut of the other's base and there's no incentive to underperform for a passive cut). Total in-band payout is exactly 1.45× mob EXP regardless of damage split. Outside the band: plain contribution EXP only, which closes power-leveling and alt-box exploits. Parties can still form at any gap; VB6 rejected party requests with a gap over 5. |
| Sub potions | `PotionSubHp`, `PotionSubMp`, and `PotionSubSp` in VB6 subtracted the player's own vitals with no benefit. They now exchange vitals PROPORTIONALLY: spending a given fraction of one pool restores half that fraction of each of the other two. Draining half your HP bar restores a quarter of your MP bar and a quarter of your SP bar, whatever their absolute sizes — SP is a much smaller pool than HP, so a raw amount would be worthless in one direction and overflow in the other. Only what is actually drained is paid for. |
| Spell mana cost rebalance | Spell mana cost is a fixed function of spell metadata — `(VitalAmount + 15)^1.5 / 6` — with Add-type spells (AddHp/AddMp/AddSp) paying a 10% tax on top. No player or class INT enters the cost, so the same spell costs the same MP at level 1 and level 100. **Two axes:** within an INT tier, damage-per-MP slopes **down** with `VitalAmount` (cantrips win efficiency, burst spells win per-cast damage); across INT tiers, damage-per-MP for any given spell slopes **up** with INT investment. Because `VitalAmount` growth tracks the quadratic MP-regen curve, the burst-vs-cantrip trade stays meaningful at endgame instead of collapsing into infinite-MP trivia: cantrips refill faster than they drain (sustainable forever) while heavy spells genuinely drain the pool. Sample costs: `VitalAmount` 3 → 12 MP, 10 → 20, 50 → 87, 100 → 205, 200 → 525, 250 → 718. VB6's `classInt` discount is removed from cost — it was an asymmetric class perk unrecoverable through gameplay; class identity now comes from the MP pool and the INT-requirement head-start. `GiveItem` costs off its own `IntReq`, since it carries no magnitude to gate on — its `ItemNum` is an id. |
| NPC parity with players | NPC damage and mitigation use the **same formulas as players at matched gear** — damage as a player's unarmed hit plus a weapon contribution, mitigation as the player base plus two implicit armor pieces, one unified value resisting physical and magic alike. Two deliberate PvE asymmetries tune the *feel* of an on-level fight without touching the shared formulas: NPCs get **up to +30% effective max HP** (ramped by physical investment, reaching its cap by mid-level) and deal a flat **−30% damage to players**, applied at the damage chokepoint for melee, spell, and crit alike and mirrored into the kill-EXP danger term. Together they make a matched mob tankier yet less lethal — impactful but not deadly 1v1, and gentler still on squishy builds. Both are player-facing only: **NPC-vs-NPC HP and damage stay full mirror**. |
| NPC virtual level (SPD = durability) | An NPC has no earned level, so it's given a **player-faithful virtual level** inferred from its point spread exactly as a player's level relates to theirs — a class starts at 20 total points and each level grants 3. That one level drives an NPC's vitals, mitigation, EXP reward, and the on-target strength readout alike (the editor shows a single "Level"). Crucially **SPD counts**: a fast NPC represents a higher-invested, higher-level character and is tankier by its level floor, exactly like a SPD-heavy player. SPD buys **durability** — more HP and mitigation, never damage — so a fast NPC reads tougher, *is* tougher, and rewards more EXP, with no hidden "reads tough but is paper" mismatch. NPC mitigation uses the same level-primary curve players use, plus a fully-kitted defender's gear baked in since NPCs wear none (armor and helmet at full weight, a shield at 1/4). The faithful slope makes low-tier mobs squishier and high-tier mobs markedly tankier — a ~350-stat boss roughly doubles its HP and triples its mitigation — so **content stat numbers want a re-tune against the new ceilings**. |
| HP/MP regen scales with pool | HP and MP regen use the same shifted-quadratic shape as their pools rather than a flat linear rate, keeping regen-per-tick at roughly 7-10% of pool across every stat tier — so post-fight recovery and in-combat sustain feel the same at level 5 or level 50. SP regen stays linear because the SP pool itself is linear. NPC HP, MP, and SP regen all mirror the player formulas at parity, so no vital-regen *amount* differs between a player and an NPC; only the tick cadence does. **Cadence:** player HP every 2.5 s out of combat (1.25 s in a safe zone, paused in combat); player MP/SP every 2.5 s out of combat (1 s in a safe zone, 10 s in combat); NPCs every 5 s flat with HP suppressed during combat — fights are decisive on both sides. |
| NPC night-boost | While it is Night, NPCs deal more damage to players (melee and spells), grant more EXP when killed, and have higher effective max HP — making good on the "NPCs grow stronger in the darkness" nightfall warning. On each Night boundary every NPC's current HP and its damage-contribution ledgers retro-scale proportionally, so each keeps its HP percentage and its EXP/aggro contribution fractions across the flip whether idle or mid-fight. The boost is strictly binary on the Night phase (Dusk and Dawn are unaffected) and applies only to NPC-vs-player combat, never NPC-vs-NPC. Magnitudes are tunable server constants — defaults: damage ×1.10, HP ×1.10, EXP ×1.20. VB6 had no time-of-day-driven difficulty. |
| Post-cast root removed | VB6 hard-blocked movement for a flat 1000 ms after every cast. That **move lock is removed entirely**, for players and NPCs. At its old value it was coterminous with the cast cooldown, so it only ever forbade *walking* during a second you already couldn't recast in; simulator sweeps found PvP win-rates flat at every lock value for equal-speed same-level matchups, and the lock only began to bite above ~2000 ms. Removing it makes same-level caster fights fluid instead of a static cast-then-freeze stat-check, and lets **NPC casters kite and cast** — back off, fire, keep backing off — rather than freezing after each bolt. The 1-second cast cadence still paces spell damage; only movement is freed. |

VB6 used `(global) Name: msg` / `(admin) Name msg` shapes, embedded the stylized text directly into the log, and prefixed Say/Yell logs with the speaker's map number, which is no longer meaningful under seamless maps. |

A press-and-release on the same link with under 3 px of drift counts as a click; a real selection sweep that starts over a link still selects normally. A URL long enough to wrap across two display rows remains a single logical link. Trailing punctuation and unbalanced close-brackets are stripped from the target, so end-of-sentence periods and "(www.example.com)" don't get glued on, while Wikipedia-style "Foo_(bar)" parens are preserved. Chat enables hyperlinks; the `/help` panel doesn't. |
| Chat timestamps + channel labels | Each chat line can show two optional bracketed bits ahead of the message — the local wall-clock time it arrived and its channel, e.g. `[2:34:07 PM] [Say] Sparrow says, 'hi'`. The timestamp is dim gray so it reads as a side column; the channel label stays in the line's base color. Two independent **per-character** options, both off by default: **Show Chat Timestamps**, with a dependent **Use 24-Hour Clock** sub-toggle (drawn grayed and inert until timestamps are on), and **Show Channel Labels**. Both time formats always carry seconds, since chat scrolls fast. The two bits compose with exact spacing whether none, one, or both are on. Channel-less lines (client-local diagnostics like `/fps`) and the un-filterable welcome/MOTD bucket carry no channel bracket even with the option on. Both bits are captured the instant a line is appended — even while hidden — so revealing either option later stamps every earlier line faithfully, and flipping the clock format reformats the whole buffer live. Player-name coloring, right-click hit-testing, and URL links stay aligned under the added prefix, and wrapping lands the prefix on the first visual row only. VB6 had neither. |

"+" adds a tab (capped at 6, hidden at the cap); each tab has an "X" to close it (hidden when only one remains). Right-clicking a tab opens per-tab options: a rename field (on-tab display truncated at 15 chars), three section-header tri-state toggles (Chat / System / Combat) above a 2-column grid of the 10 channels, and a "Flash on new message" toggle that pulses the tab until clicked. The Admin row is absent entirely for non-admins. All tab state persists per account. Server-side, every chat send site tags an explicit channel — 11 in total, with **Always** (welcome, help hint, MOTD, players-online) bypassing every filter so the welcome batch can't be muted. Within the System group, **Notice** carries server-wide announcements and admin actions, **System** carries personal feedback about your own character (level up/down, milestones, durability loss), and **Join / Leave** is split out so it can be muted separately. In the Combat group, **Combat** is the live hit-by-hit feed while **Rewards** is the EXP-and-loot ledger — and their defaults differ on purpose: the General tab keeps Rewards but hides the noisier Combat feed, while the dedicated Combat tab shows both. |
| Chat bubbles | Say, Yell, and Broadcast messages from players — plus AttackSay from NPCs — render as a stylized rounded-rect bubble with a drop shadow above the speaker, alongside the chat-log line. Each channel uses a distinct border color (silver Say, yellow Yell, pink Broadcast, red hostile NPC, green friendly or shopkeeper NPC) so speech reads at a glance. Visible duration scales with message length (~200 WPM, clamped 2.5-8 s); when a bubble expires or is replaced it detaches and drifts upward while fading, and rapid-fire speech stacks drifters into a rising column. Bubbles track the speaker as they walk and survive map-seam crossings so they don't blink off mid-chase, and if the speaker dies mid-bubble the last words still drift away from the corpse tile. Broadcast bubbles ship to every connected player but render only when the speaker is in your viewport, so someone who walks into range mid-life still sees the remainder. Other chat types (emote, whisper, notice, admin, roll) stay chat-log-only. The party overlay picks up the same drop-shadow treatment for consistency. |

Each element clamps independently, so a wide chat bubble can shift more than the narrow HP bar above it and each stays inside the view. When the label group flips below the sprite — an actor near the top of the screen — chat bubbles follow the name to the underside and their drifters float downward instead of clipping out the top. |

Admin commands are a section of the one `/help` panel, shown to whoever qualifies. A **channel dropdown** left of the chat input sets which channel plain text goes to — Say / Yell / Broadcast always, plus Admin, Guild, and Officer when you qualify. It opens upward so the bottom-docked chat can't clip it, falls back to Say if you stop qualifying, and persists per character. Explicit slash commands override the dropdown for that one message — **except** a channel command typed with **no message** (e.g. `/g`, `/y`), which instead **switches the dropdown** to that channel so you can change your default from the keyboard. Right-click **Whisper** and `/r` reply prefill `/w <name> `. VB6's `"`-prefixed Global admin broadcast is now `/notice`. |
| Chat color palette | Every chat channel and overhead-name rank reads as a **distinct, legible** color on the near-black chat background. VB6's 16-color palette is preserved verbatim; every new color is appended and named for the color itself, with role aliases mapping a channel or rank onto a color so all of its send sites recolor from one place. Developer name → royal blue, mapper → turquoise, player name → warm tan, monitor → orange, creator → amethyst, warning/error → coral, admin **Notice** → periwinkle; emote shares Say's gray, **Roll** → cornflower, **admin chat** → rose. The guild and war channels, which had collided on shared slots, get their own families: guild chat emerald, officer chat a lighter mint (the senior channel reads brighter), public war crimson, private guild-war brick. **NPC dialogue** decouples from the player-name slot to its own olive-gold. Every labeled channel and name rank is mutually distinct, locked by a test; the one intentional overlap is a Player-Killer's name sharing combat's pure red. |
| Movement | Movement uses delta-time interpolation at VB6-exact speeds. Rejected moves animate a smooth snap-back. All actions share a single cooldown timer; holding a key repeats the action. |
| Movement input stack (last-pressed wins) | Holding several movement keys steps in the direction of the **most recently pressed** one, and releasing it falls back to whatever key is still held — a press-order stack rather than a fixed priority. Hold **W** to walk up, add **D** and you turn right, release **D** and you resume up without ever lifting **W**; the stack unwinds in reverse press-order for any depth. Keys that first appear on the same tick, where there's no real order to read, fall back to a fixed Up > Down > Left > Right precedence. VB6 set the last-pressed key on key-*down* but on key-*up* cleared only that one flag without restoring any still-held key, so releasing the top key stopped the character even with another direction held. The same press-order resolution covers the gamepad D-pad and the **Ctrl+WASD face-only channel**, which runs its own stack, tracked continuously so the key order is already current the instant Ctrl is pressed. |
| NPC movement & chase | NPCs glide continuously instead of stepping and freezing, at one tile per 500 ms — slower than a walking player's 400 ms, so a player can always step away from a walking NPC. Idle NPCs **wander in committed strides**: on a 1-in-8 cadence they pick a heading and amble 2-5 tiles, each step with a small chance to bend a right angle but never reverse; wander is map-confined. **NPCs run when they chase**, at a **flat baseline** — the same 5 tiles/sec a 0-SPD player runs — while they have stamina. Run speed is deliberately **not** scaled by SPD: a player outruns any chaser purely by investing speed, and an NPC's SPD instead buys run *duration*. A mob that empties its SP **rebuilds a 50% reservoir at a walk** before sprinting again. **Approach depends on distance:** a target spotted far off is rushed at a run, one spotted close is stalked in at a walk (1-in-5 chance it charges anyway), and a stalked target who opens the gap gets charged; a mob hit before it closed skips the walk-in and sprints. After contact it does **not** glue itself to you — it follows at a **walk** while you stay close and runs only when you open a **~3-tile gap**, so it bursts stamina rather than pinning itself at melee range draining SP. The one-tile walk band lets you slip *past* a mob, but only a running player opens real distance. **Guards** and **spell-primary casters** are exempt and always run. On arrival a chaser finishes its slide, then turns to face you before swinging. Chasers pursue **blind** — pathing on walls alone with no knowledge of other actors — and bump-adjust reactively. Pace carries identically across map seams. |
| Combat cooldown unification | Player attack, NPC attack, and spell cast share the same 1000 ms beat. |
| Weather system | A global weather cycle — new; VB6 defined weather data but never implemented the logic — runs five states: Clear, Rain, Heat Wave, Snow, and Heavy Wind. An idle timer (1-2 h) rolls a 40% chance to trigger a weighted-random non-Clear state (Rain 50%, Heat Wave and Snow 20% each, Heavy Wind 10%) for a per-type duration, then returns to Clear. Effects apply to players and NPCs alike. **Rain** doubles equipment durability loss (per-hit wear and the chunk lost on death) *and* the casting-reagent cost of damage spells — the warrior's and caster's weather upkeep in lockstep. **Heat Wave** halves all vital regen and doubles every stamina cost. **Snow** halves regen and temporarily shrinks max vitals (HP −10%, MP/SP −20%), scaling current vitals proportionally so nothing overfills or gaps. **Heavy Wind** tears a flat share of attacks and casts off course before they land (an attacker-side miss, so the target spends no stamina avoiding it and the attacker still pays the cost and the cooldown), prevents every stamina-based proc (block, dodge, and physical and spell crit cannot occur at all), and doubles the whole combat beat. Any non-Clear weather grants bonus EXP (Rain +5%, Heat Wave and Snow +15%, Heavy Wind +25%) compounding multiplicatively with the night and party bonuses. Weather persists across restarts and pauses while the server is offline. A `/weather <state>` developer command forces any state and announces the shift publicly while privately telling staff who caused it. The HUD shows the weather beside the time of day (e.g. "Windy Night", with a countdown on hover), and a yellow cooldown bar appears under any caster's sprite after each spell. |
| Collision | Players and NPCs block each other from entering occupied tiles, except in safe zones where players walk through one another — useful for un-jamming crowded shop tiles and town entrances. The exemption follows the seam: if either side of the move is a safe map, pass-through applies. PK-flagged players are the lone exception, colliding with other players in safe zones too. NPC blocking is unchanged regardless of map type, and NPCs spawn only on unoccupied, walkable tiles. VB6 always blocked player-on-player movement everywhere. |
| Variable-size NPCs | An NPC can be authored at one of three **sizes**: 1 = 32×32 (one tile, as before), 2 = 64×64 (2×2 tiles), 3 = 96×96 (3×3 tiles). A larger NPC genuinely **occupies its whole footprint**, anchored at its top-left tile: players and other NPCs are blocked from every tile it stands on, it spawns only where the full block is clear, and its pathing only plans onto cells where the whole body fits. Its **melee strikes the entire leading edge** — a swing hits every tile immediately in front of the footprint in the facing direction, so several players lined up against a giant all take the blow from one swing. Magic is unchanged, still a radius-5 circle. Bars, overhead name, target arrow, and click-targeting all scale to the body. A big NPC can **straddle a map seam**, blocking from both sides, and follows a fleeing player across it like any chaser. Sprites load per size from `assets/graphics/sprites/{32x32,64x64,96x96}/`; a missing size sheet draws no sprite (bars and collision still work) until art is added. VB6 NPCs were always a single tile. |
| Doors & cross-seam keys | A locked-door (Key) tile paints its closed-door art over a walkable floor. While unlocked the client hides that art — revealing the floor beneath — then restores it when the door re-locks 5 seconds after **that** door opened. Door state — the open flag and the auto-close clock alike — is tracked per (tile, layer), with a snapshot on join, so doors render correctly even on the neighboring maps shown in the seamless view. Using a key on a locked door **across a seam** now works: the faced tile resolves in world coordinates against the 3×3 observable area, the same way melee, spells, and line-of-sight already reach across seams, so a door one tile past the map edge is no longer skipped. VB6 had no map seams, so keys only ever acted within a single map. |
| Spells | All spell types scale with the magic stat and can land critical hits. A Prepared hotkey (Q) casts without opening the spells panel; spells show cost, INT requirement, and prepared status inline. Failed casts report the specific reason. Beneficial spells bypass PvP zone restrictions. Self-targeting and no-target casting are handled gracefully; GiveItem delivers to the targeted player. Casting while moving is permitted — the current step completes, then the player is immobilized for one second, and only a *successful* cast triggers it, so failed casts never interrupt movement. VB6 rejected casts while walking. A red **Forget** button unlearns the selected spell after an in-panel confirmation reminding the player they can relearn it by using the source item again; the slot clears in place and auto-unprepares if it was prepared, and the original item is not refunded. Learning and forgetting are both combat-locked; VB6 had no forget affordance and let players learn mid-fight. |
| Self-cast (Ctrl+Cast) | Holding **Ctrl** while casting — via the **Q** hotkey or the spell panel's Cast button — targets the caster for that one cast without changing the selected target, so a beneficial spell lands on you mid-fight while your enemy stays locked for the next normal cast. Heal and buff spells resolve on you and a GiveItem spell hands its item to you; a harmful spell aimed at yourself is still refused. This complements the persistent self-target (**Ctrl+Tab**), which instead switches your selected target to yourself. |
| GiveItem spell cost | VB6 derived MP cost and level requirement from `Data1`, which for GiveItem spells is the item index — so giving a high-index item demanded hundreds of MP and a matching level, while a low-index item was nearly free. GiveItem now carries a dedicated `IntReq` that sets both its cost and its INT requirement; `ItemNum` is a pure identifier contributing nothing to cost, and `ItemAmount` is how many. Non-GiveItem formulas are unchanged — those spells have a real `VitalAmount`, and "stronger spell = more mana" is intentional. |
| Spell line of sight | A straight tile-line is traced from caster to target; if it crosses any Blocked tile or a closed Key door the cast fails with "You don't have line of sight!" and the target arrow renders gray instead of red. Movement is cardinal-only, so a diagonal corner with Blocked tiles on *both* perpendicular cells counts as an impassable wall even though the line slips between them. The check honors live door state and works across map seams. VB6 had no LoS check — any target inside the caster's viewport could be hit through any wall. |
| NPC combat | NPCs can land critical hits and block attacks. Aggro follows the highest **DEF-weighted** damage contributor — a tankier attacker holds threat over a squishier one dealing similar damage — and is **sticky**: the current target is kept unless a challenger's weighted damage pulls decisively past a margin, so aggro no longer flickers between evenly-matched attackers on every hit. Aggressive NPCs initially acquire the lowest-level player in range. NPCs fully restore HP and clear damage history on disengage, preventing chip-damage abuse. A player's death removes their contributions from all NPCs on the map. |
| NPC magic | Every aggressive NPC can cast in principle, just as every NPC can swing — capability is implicit from the INT stat. An INT=0 NPC never casts; an INT>0 NPC **weaves** melee and magic, rolling cast-vs-melee on each ready beat at `P(cast) = Int/(Int+Str)`, so the INT:STR blend an author gives a mob *is* its cast:melee blend. Spell magnitude is the exact mirror of its melee swing evaluated on INT, rolled with the same ±10% variance and reduced by the target's unified mitigation. The spell fires from inside the R=5 circle and needs line of sight and mana exactly like player spells; each cast prints `A <NpcName> casts a spell on <Target>.` to every observer in the caster's viewport, with no spell name since NPC spells aren't record-backed. A cast costs a trivial pool fraction and no reagent, and in-combat regen out-paces that drain, so a caster **sustains its spell attack** rather than running dry. A mob that can't afford a cast falls back to melee regardless of the roll — there is no "wait for mana" state — so a caster whose pool is driven near-empty (by Snow, say) swings instead of standing idle. Spell crits use the same chance and SP cost as NPC melee crits. **Kiting is archetype-gated:** only an **INT-primary** caster kites, casting in place at safe range and retreating one zig-zag step that preserves range and LoS when the target closes, then giving up after 3 failed retreats and casting at melee range anyway — so a player can't lock a mage into never casting by chasing it. A **STR-primary or balanced** mob does **not** kite: it fights at melee range and casts from there, keeping its DPS steady either way. Both **commit to whichever modality they roll for 3-5 beats** before re-rolling rather than flipping every beat, and an INT-primary hybrid casts more when its pool is deep, shifting toward melee as it drains. Friendly and Stationary NPCs are excluded, as they are for player spells. |
| Guard NPCs | Guards proactively hunt PK-flagged players across the whole visible region, pursuing across map borders, and also acquire any player who initiates PvP before they receive the PK flag, releasing if combat expires peacefully. Guards patrol and remove player-dropped litter — but only items dropped **voluntarily** from the inventory panel. Items shed by a player who dies in a safe zone are tagged separately and the janitor sweep ignores them, so the victim gets a window to recover their gear and others can loot the corpse — the same exemption NPC loot piles already had. VB6 guards swept everything, so a player killed in town saw their drops vanish the moment a guard walked over them. Guards also ignore the **NpcAvoid** map attribute entirely — both route planning and the per-step move check treat it as walkable — so npc-block barriers placed to fence wild mobs out of an area don't impede a guard chasing a PK or sweeping litter. Real `Blocked` walls, warps, and closed Key doors still stop a guard like any NPC, and occupied tiles still block; only NpcAvoid is bypassed, and only for guards. VB6 applied the walkable test uniformly, so guards there were fenced out like everything else. |
| NPC vs NPC combat | NPCs can attack and kill each other, using the same melee and spell systems they use against players. Two paths trigger it: guards acquire any hostile mob currently chasing a player within their viewport (no LoS gate, matching the PK scan), and AttackOnSight mobs acquire a different-kind hostile NPC within their Range (LoS-gated, like their player scan). Same-kind AoS mobs never fight each other; Friendly, Stationary, and Guard NPCs are never targeted as victims, though a guard can become one via aggro flip after engaging. A player target always outranks an NPC target. **Safe-zone scope:** the protection redirect that pulls a dragged mob onto a viewport guard fires for **player** targets only, so a mob that wanders into town mid-fight with another NPC keeps that fight and guards never hijack an NPC brawl that spills into a safe zone. Once an AoS mob commits it fights to the death; only aggro re-evaluation can switch its target. Guards keep their PK priority swap and pre-empt any NPC pursuit the moment a PK appears, and comrade guards of the same kind within the engaging guard's viewport wake to the same target, correctly across seams. NPC damage is tracked in a parallel ledger: **if any NPC dealt more damage than every player on a kill, no loot drops at all**, so players can't cheese free loot by letting a guard finish; player EXP still scales by damage share, so a player who tags for 1 damage earns ~1% of the EXP. A dead contributor's credit is purged from every other NPC's ledger, so a respawn into the same slot starts clean rather than inheriting the dead one's damage. NPCs broadcast their AttackSay to every observer when engaging an NPC target; NPC-vs-player AttackSay stays private to the target. Cross-seam chase works exactly as it does against a player, and magic NPCs cast at NPC targets with the same kite-or-hold behavior. The full crit / block / dodge cascade runs in both directions, melee and magic alike, at the same NPC caps (block 25%, dodge 10%, crit 25%) and SP costs used against a player. |
| AoS NPC group alliances | Each NPC template carries an editor-assigned **Group** tag (1-based; 0 = ungrouped). An AttackOnSight mob will not attack another NPC sharing its non-zero group, on top of the existing same-kind exclusion — so a designer can build a mixed-type allied pack that doesn't infight without making them the literal same NPC. The rule is symmetric and additive; group 0 preserves the original same-kind-only behavior exactly, so existing NPCs are unaffected. It's scoped to AoS self-targeting only — guards still defend players against any hostile mob regardless of group — and because all NPC damage is single-target, same-group mobs can never damage or retaliate against each other once acquisition is blocked. |
| NPC pathing | NPCs run a breadth-first search each tick across the whole 3×3 observable area (1,728 world tiles) to find the shortest walkable route to their target, routing around walls, U-shapes, and arbitrary geometry, and traversing linked map borders along the way. VB6 stepped a single cardinal axis toward the target and stopped at the first L-corner or pillar. Same-map and cross-map chases flow through one routine; unlinked grid cells are treated as walls. The chase plans **blind to live actors** by default — static geometry only, resolving collisions reactively — but masks the target's occupied attack-slots so a lined-up trailer routes to an open flank. A chaser that **stalls** behind a mid-path blocker re-plans occupancy-aware and routes around it, except when the blocker is itself chasing the chaser (a guard deliberately walling its target off), which it holds behind rather than dancing around its own hunter — so the plan stays consistent across ticks and doesn't oscillate. NPCs give up only when no walkable path exists across the observable area. A step that succeeds but doesn't close world-distance to the target — a perpendicular slide along a wall — no longer counts as progress for the give-up clock, so an NPC pacing an impassable barrier eventually times out instead of pacing forever. |
| AttackOnSight give-up | An AttackOnSight NPC that goes **10 seconds** without landing any damaging action on its target — no melee, no chase step, no cast — drops the lock and returns to scanning. That only fires when the NPC genuinely cannot act: pinned by terrain, or a caster out of mana that also cannot path. A high-INT mob casting from range is still engaging and keeps its lock, so ranged combat is not penalized. VB6's aggro held until the target left the map. |
| Target acquisition: line of sight, reachability & nearest | AttackOnSight NPCs acquire only players they can actually **see** — the same straight-line trace spells use, blocked by walls and closed Key doors. The scan filters in-range observers by LoS and picks the lowest-level survivor, so a player behind a pillar isn't aggro'd and the next-lowest visible player is picked instead. Once acquired, the NPC chases through walls as before — LoS gates acquisition, not pursuit, so ducking behind a wall after being seen doesn't break aggro. Acquisition also enforces a **reachability gate**: a candidate that passes LoS but has no walkable path (behind an NpcAvoid wall, sealed in an alcove, on a disconnected island) is skipped and the next reachable candidate is picked, which prevents a give-up-then-instantly-reacquire loop on an unreachable target. Within the unchanged priority order, an NPC acquires the **nearest** eligible candidate rather than the first one reached in scan order, so it no longer locks onto a creature across the region over one standing right next to it; ties fall back to first-seen. Mobs-vs-players keep the intentional lowest-level "prey on the weak" priority but break **equal-level** ties by distance. Guards are deliberately exempt from the LoS and reachability gates — they stay vigilant across the whole observable area regardless of walls — and now rush the nearest threat. VB6 had no LoS check for aggro; any player inside range was acquired through walls. |
| Durability condition coloring | Every `cur/max` durability readout is color-coded on one shared scale — white above 75% of max, yellow above 25%, red at or below (a broken 0/max piece reads red). The equipment paper-doll prints each equipped piece's durability beneath the stat bonus it grants, so wear is visible without opening a tooltip; the item tooltip, the repair panel, and the shop buy/trade preview all use identical coding, so a worn or broken piece reads the same everywhere. The coding is kept deliberately separate from the durability degrade-chance bands, so retuning wear rates never silently shifts the colors. VB6 had no equipment panel and no durability coloring. |
| PK system | Each kill adds 60 minutes to a real-time countdown that persists across logout. Being killed by another player reduces the timer by 30 minutes, so two deaths clear a full 60-minute flag. Partial and full clears each broadcast a distinct server-wide message. All attackers earn EXP proportional to damage dealt when a player is killed, and the stats panel shows remaining time while flagged. VB6 made the PK flag permanent until cleared by a single player kill. |
| Aggressor flag | Throwing the first hit at a non-PK, non-aggressor target marks the attacker as an aggressor for 30 seconds — even on 0-damage, blocked, or dodged hits, and on harmful spells. The flag refreshes on every combat hit given or received, and clears on death, on becoming a PKer, or on natural lapse. While lit: guards target the player exactly as they would a PKer, the player can be attacked in safe zones, and a player who kills them earns no PK flag — the victim still takes normal non-PKer death penalties. Counter-attacking an aggressor doesn't flag the retaliator, matching the rule for hitting back at a PKer. Observers render the aggressor's name as a flashing red/yellow overlay, distinct from solid-red PK, so a hostile-but-not-yet-PK player is visually unambiguous. |
| Safe zones | Entering or leaving a safe zone delivers a colored base line of its own — green on entry, red on exit. Level 10+ players receive the PvP-implications note as a separate gray line beneath it; PK-flagged players get a status-specific gray note on entry, since they remain attackable regardless of map type. |
| Arena map moral | A third map **moral** (`None` / `Safe` / **Arena**) for consequence-free PvP — a place to test damage and skills, or an in-game duel ground. A player-vs-player kill involving an Arena carries **no stakes**: the loser loses no EXP, drops no items, and takes no durability damage; the killer gains no EXP and is not flagged PK or aggressor. The waiver applies whenever **either** combatant stands on an Arena map, so a duelist can't be punished by an opponent standing just across a border. Combat is free on the same "either party" basis: in a player-vs-player fight, worn equipment takes no durability wear and damage spells consume no reagents. This is strictly PvP — fighting NPCs wears gear and costs reagents normally on every map, arena included. In every other respect an Arena behaves like an open map: players collide, PK grace breaks normally, regen runs at the open-world cadence, **Arena↔Safe combat stays blocked exactly like None↔Safe**, and the admin and level-10 PvP gates are still enforced. Entering announces "You step into the arena."; leaving says "You exit the arena." The kill is announced only to the arena's observers and the two duelists — "X has triumphed over Y in a duel." — never on the global feed, and the loser privately reads "You were bested in the arena, but lost nothing." The HUD renders an arena's name in yellow. VB6 had only None and Safe. |
| Death broadcast flavor | Kill-feed announcements were reworded and, for mob kills, made relative-strength aware. A player-vs-player kill reads "X has been murdered by Y". When an NPC kills a player the broadcast varies by the same virtual-level gap as the on-target strength readout: a mob at least 5 levels above the victim "has slaughtered" them, a mob at least 5 levels below means the victim "let their guard down and was killed by" it, and anything in between "has been killed by a" it. All kill-feed lines end with terminal punctuation. Flavor only — no penalty, reward, or mechanic changed. |
| Level 10 milestone notice | On reaching level 10 — the threshold where death penalties and open PvP both turn on — the player sees three extra messages after the normal level-up line: a yellow congratulation, a red warning that EXP loss and de-leveling are now in effect, and a red note that they can now attack and be attacked outside safe zones. Surfaces the two silent rule changes at the moment they activate instead of leaving the player to discover them by dying. |
| Barter and trade are different words | A shop exchanging one item for another is a **barter**; an exchange between two players is a **trade**. VB6 used `/trade` for the NPC shop and had no player-to-player exchange at all, so the two ideas shared a word for something only one of them did. Buying, selling and bartering all accept a **quantity** rather than one unit at a time, and a currency line can be bought in part. |
| Shops and inventory | Shops display the player's current gold balance, and repair shows the cost before deducting. Inventory entries show durability, stat requirements, and equipped status inline, and clicking a ground item reports its name and quantity. The inventory panel also shows a right-justified `used / max` slot counter beside the gold readout, colored by capacity (white below 50%, yellow below 90%, red at 90%+) for at-a-glance pressure on when to sell or drop. |
| Bank | Players can deposit and withdraw items and gold at Inn-type shops with banking enabled. The 200-slot bank panel opens automatically when the server confirms access, showing inventory on the left and bank contents on the right. Currency shows an amount field; other items move in full. Depositing an equipped item is blocked with a message to unequip first. A **[Sort]** link tidies the vault into the same canonical order as the inventory sort. The bank is **shared across the whole account** — one vault every character deposits into and withdraws from — and persists across sessions. VB6 defined the bank data structure but never implemented access logic or a UI. |
| Inn | Shops have a type: Store (classic trade/repair) or Inn. The Inn panel opens when the innkeeper is spoken to, and every action it offers re-checks that the player is still within interaction range of that keeper; step away and it reports "Not close enough to use the inn." The Inn panel offers **Set Spawn**, **Access Bank** (enabled only when the inn's Allow Banking flag is set), and the marketplace. Store actions — trade, repair, and the shop open request — are rejected at Inns and vice versa. All flags are editable per-shop in the editor: a Store/Inn radio group controls type, an Allow Banking checkbox appears only for Inns, and the trade grid and Fixes Items checkbox are hidden for Inns. VB6 had no Inn concept; shop type was implicit in how the map was wired. |
| Shop entry chatter | Stepping onto a shop map prints a gray "You walk into a shop." or "You walk into an inn." before the shopkeeper's JoinSay, so the player gets unambiguous feedback even when the shop has no JoinSay configured. Entry and the existing JoinSay/LeaveSay messages are gated by **shop number, not map number**: walking between contiguous maps that share the same logical shop stays silent, so chatter fires only when actually entering, leaving, or moving between different shops. VB6 printed only the optional NPC greeting and fired its leave/join lines on every map seam, so a multi-map shop chattered on every internal step. |
| `/home` and `/homecd` | `/home` warps a character to their home point on a **30-minute cooldown** measured in wall-clock time from a stamp on the character, so it runs down while they are logged out and a relog neither clears nor pauses it. `/homecd` asks how long is left and writes nothing. The cooldown is stamped only **after** the destination checks pass, so a home point naming no tile refuses the warp without burning the cooldown. Blocked in combat. VB6 had no recall of any kind. |
| Spawn point | Players can set a custom respawn location at any Inn. **Set Spawn** shows a gold-cost confirm overlay (cost = ⌈level^1.25 × 5⌉, minimum 5) displaying current gold and the cost before committing. Confirming deducts the gold, records the current position as the spawn, and saves the character. On death the player warps to their custom spawn if set, else the default start location. VB6 had no custom spawn point; players always respawned at the hardcoded start tile. |
| Item world | Each spawn tile has an independent respawn timer that fires regardless of player presence, and dropped items persist across server restarts. Map ground capacity is no longer hard-capped at 20 slots: voluntary drops still respect the 20-item clutter limit — counted only against player-dropped items, so NPC loot piles never lock anyone out — but death drops and NPC loot land regardless of clutter. VB6 silently lost NPC loot and death-drop overflow once the map's 20 slots filled. |
| Combat feedback | Damage, healing, and EXP gains float upward from entities and fade; critical hits are marked, a blocked or dodged hit floats a cyan **Blocked!!** / **Dodged!!** over the defender, and a hit that mitigates to 0 floats a gray **0 HP** / **0 MP** / **0 SP** naming the drained vital. Every nearby player — including anyone watching from an adjacent map — sees the same feedback simultaneously. World-space HP/MP/SP bars are drawn above entities. Floats and world bars have independent in-game toggles. <br><br>**Combat state** floats too: an orange **Enter Combat** the moment the player enters the 10 s combat window, and a green **End Combat** when it expires — the same threshold the HUD bars and quit-confirm use. It fires for the local player and the party partner, anchored over the partner's live sprite when visible. Beneficial spells don't flip combat status by themselves: a heal lands the caster in combat only when the target is already in combat, so casting heals in peaceful situations no longer raises the combat bar. Damage spells still mark combat on cast. |
| NPC information | NPC names are drawn above sprites. Clicking a hostile NPC shows a strength comparison alongside the target confirmation. |
| Tab to target | Tab cycles attackable entities across the whole visible region — the player's map and its neighbors — in order of distance, closest first; Shift+Tab cycles backward, and Ctrl+Tab targets the local player for beneficial self-casts without the mouse. On safe maps only AttackOnSight and AttackWhenAttacked NPCs are included; players are excluded by default, toggleable in Options. Entities with no spell line of sight are skipped, so the cycle only lands on things you could actually cast on. Targeting mirrors a tile click and works on any entity you can see, not just those on your own map. A red arrow with a hover animation marks the current target, appearing above or below the entity name depending on screen position and graying out when the target is beyond casting range or has no line of sight. The lock is tracked by identity, so it follows a targeted NPC across a border and clears only when the entity dies, gives up, or leaves the observable area. Clicking an empty tile also clears the target. |
| Opportunistic target acquisition | The click scan that prints level-diff readouts and item listings stays server-side, but the *target* a click acquires is picked client-side from the rendered viewport — a sprite-pixel hit test against what the player actually sees, for every target type including self. Moving entities mid-step occupy two tiles visually, so a click on either tile now lands; VB6 matched only the entity's destination tile against the server's authoritative position, so half of every step was unclickable. The client sends its picked identity alongside the clicked tile and the server validates it before emitting the usual target and strength-compare messages; if validation fails the server sends a target-clear and the client drops the stale guess. |
| Combat ghost | A player who disconnects while in combat leaves a ghost body in the world. The ghost persists until the combat timer expires or the character dies, with all normal death penalties applying, and the player can reconnect and resume control within that window. VB6 removed the player immediately on disconnect, allowing death-penalty evasion. |
| Corpses & the timed dead state | Dying no longer warps you straight back to a spawn tile. Your body stays in the world as a walk-over-able red-X **corpse** behind an uncloseable respawn panel, counting down an **escalating, decaying, capped** timer — die repeatedly and the wait grows, leave it alone and the penalty sheds. A corpse "does not exist" as a combat entity: it cannot be attacked, damaged, or killed by NPCs or players, cannot be healed or buffed, and cannot act — movement, turning, melee, casting, item pickup/drop/use, shop trade and repair, banking, stat training, and spell prepare/forget are all frozen server-side, leaving only chat, logout, and Respawn. NPCs skip corpses during target acquisition — both the lowest-level scan and the guard PK scan, since death doesn't clear the PK flag — and every NPC damage path refuses a dead victim, so the countdown can't be reset by repeated hits. Dying in combat also sends the same green "The heat of battle fades." notice a natural combat expiry produces. A dead player can always **log out**: the HUD Logout button and Escape both reach the quit dialog while dead, and the server takes the normal-leave path for a corpse — persisting the dead state so a relogin re-opens the death panel with the remaining countdown — rather than leaving it as a combat ghost. Guild-war participants get a flat timer and a war-area respawn in place of the escalating one. VB6 had no corpse and no dead state; death respawned you instantly. |
| Gamepad support | Xbox and PlayStation controllers work plug-and-go, no configuration required. D-pad and left stick move; B/Circle holds to run; A/Cross picks up items; X/Square attacks; Y/Triangle casts the prepared spell; LB/L1 cycles tab-target forward, RB/R1 backward, and holding both bumpers targets self. The triggers are reserved as the potion modifier — see the Controls panel for the face-button mapping. Input isn't locked to the first controller: pressing a button on any connected, idle pad instantly makes it the active one, though only one ever drives the game at a time — handy for couch play on a docked handheld where the native controls are out of reach. |
| Face direction without moving | Hold `Ctrl` + WASD on keyboard, or push the right stick on gamepad, to rotate the sprite without stepping. Holding several WASD keys faces the most-recently-pressed one and falls back to a still-held key on release, the same press-order stack movement uses. Ctrl outranks Shift so a co-held Shift can't leak a Running intent on the next non-Ctrl frame, and the right stick is suppressed entirely while the left stick or D-pad is past deadzone — face-aim never fights the direction the player is already committing to. |
| Loot tagging | NPC-dropped items are claimed by the highest damage contributor for 30 seconds. Players within 5% of the top contribution enter a roll-off; tied rolls re-roll until a single winner is found, and all tied players see roll results in chat, preceded by an `<NPC> drops <Item>.` announcement so the drop and the rolls read as one event. Currency drops name the quantity instead, since the amount is what matters. The single-tagger case also gets the announcement. The claim expires automatically and anyone can pick up the item afterward. |
| NPC drop **tables** | An NPC carries a `Drops` list — zero or more lines, each naming an item, a quantity (currency only) and a chance — instead of VB6's single item-and-chance pair. **Every line rolls independently on a kill**, so one death can yield nothing, one thing, or several: a bandit that usually drops a little gold, sometimes a potion and very rarely its sword is three lines at three rates, which the single field could not express at all. Authoring an empty table is the ordinary way to say "drops nothing". The loot **tag** still resolves once per corpse rather than per item — the roll decides who has priority on that kill's loot, so a table is never split between two players — and the guild double-drop perk rolls per line, so one lucky sword doesn't also double the gold. A pre-table record is migrated on load: its single drop folds into the table and the old fields are cleared, so the file stops being legacy the next time it is saved. <br><br>A line's `Chance` is also **a direct percentage** now — `0` never drops, `1` = 1%, `50` = 50%, `100` = always, and anything above 100 caps at 100%. VB6 treated the stored number as a *1-in-N* divisor, so `2` meant 50%, `5` meant 20%, and `100` meant 1% — bigger numbers meant *rarer* drops, which is unintuitive to author. Every shipped NPC's value was converted to preserve its old effective rate; the one NPC encoding a sub-1% chance floors to the new 1% minimum, since 0 is reserved for "never". |
| `/roll` command | Rolls a random number between 1 and a given maximum (default 100, max 255) and broadcasts the result to all players on the map. |
| Player name targeting | Commands that take a player name (`/warpmeto`, `/warptome`, `/kick`, `/ban`, `/setaccess`, tells, `/join`) require an exact, whole-name match. VB6 matched on prefix — `/warpto Rob` would warp to "Robert" — convenient when names are unique but dangerous for destructive admin actions and ambiguous when names share a prefix. |
| Party commands | `/party <name>` and `/join` are collapsed into a single dual-role `/join`. `/join <name>` sends an invite; if the named player has already invited *you*, it's treated as an acceptance rather than overwriting both sides' state. `/join` with no arguments accepts a pending invite, and `/leave` declines an invite or ungroups from an active party. VB6's separate `/party` + `/join` flow deadlocked when two players invited each other simultaneously — both ended up flagged as the inviter and neither could complete the join. |
| Named item and spell fields | VB6 packed every type-specific value into three positional slots, `Data1`/`Data2`/`Data3`, whose meaning was decoded per `ItemType` — the same number was a sword's durability, a potion's healing, and a scroll's spell id. Nothing type-checked, the field name told a reader nothing, and two independent decoders (the editor's label switch and the client tooltip) each held half the spec. Items now carry `Durability`, `VitalAmount`, `SpellNum`, `Power` and `ClassReq`; spells carry `VitalAmount`, `ItemNum`, `ItemAmount` and `IntReq` beside the `ClassReq` they already had. **`Power` stays one field** where the slot genuinely was one concept — it is a weapon's damage, a defensive piece's mitigation, the stat needed to equip it, and its repair rate, so splitting it would only force the repair and wear paths to switch on type. Which fields a type uses is stated once on the record; the editor asks it what to show and `Normalize()` asks it what to clear, so **retyping an item zeroes the fields it no longer uses** rather than leaving a potion silently carrying a weapon's `Power` — a hazard the old format had no way to express, let alone prevent. The server normalizes on save too, so a hand-built packet can't reintroduce one. Unused fields are omitted from the JSON entirely, so a row lists exactly the properties it has. |
| Class gating, on a set of classes | Items, spells and quests all restrict themselves to a set of classes, held as `AllowedClasses` — a list of class ids, where empty or absent means every class. VB6 (and the port until now) carried a single class id in three different spellings, so a piece of gear could be locked to exactly one class or none at all; there was no way to say "any of the three Strength classes", which is what a class design with groups actually needs. All three now share one field name, one `ClassGate` helper deciding who may use what, and one multi-select in the editor. **A list rather than a bit mask on purpose:** `Constants.MaxClasses` is a knob, and a mask would cap the world at whatever its integer holds — 15 in a `short`, 31 in an `int`, 63 in a `long` — failing quietly the first time that ceiling was raised past it. A list also reads as itself in a data file (`[1, 2, 3]`, not a `7` to decode bit by bit). The cost is a scan of a handful of entries on equip, learn and quest acceptance, none of them hot paths. Saving canonicalizes the set — deduped, sorted, invalid ids dropped, empty collapsed to absent — so two rows allowing the same classes are identical on disk, and the server re-normalizes whatever a client sends. Tooltips and shop lines list the allowed classes; a class-locked quest stays invisible to everyone outside the set, as before. |
| Minimum-damage floors, and a higher one for bosses | Every landed hit deals at least a fraction of its raw whatever the defender's mitigation, so stacked DEF is never full immunity and a weak attacker can always chip. Three floors, each earning its place: **12%** universally; **35%** for a player hitting an NPC, because a low-offense hybrid whose raw sits under a tanky mob's mitigation would otherwise grind roughly ten times a pure's kill time; and **19% for a boss hitting a player**. VB6 had no floor at all — mitigation subtracted flat and a sufficiently armored character simply stopped taking damage. <br><br>The boss floor exists because a dedicated tank's mitigation outgrows NPC damage at every level: a level-255 Knight carries ~1898 mitigation against a boss's ~1385 raw, so *every* boss hit lands on the floor and nothing about the fight can pressure them — the heavy armor tier they paid for stops mattering at the margin, and boss damage tuning has no effect at all. Raising the floor for bosses alone restores that pressure, and it is surgical: the floor only binds once mitigation has already eaten ~81% of the raw, which is exactly and only a tank. An ordinary player's damage taken is unchanged, because subtraction still wins for them. Measured effect at level 255: a tank takes 166/s → 263/s, and survives 170s → 107s. Every NPC-to-player path — melee and spell, crit and ordinary — routes through one `ResolveNpcVsPlayerDamage`, so the boss floor cannot apply on some swings and not others. |
| Level requirements | Items and spells carry a `LevelReq` — the minimum character level to equip, use, learn or cast. VB6 had a `LevelReq` byte on spells that nothing ever read (see the fixed-bugs list); this one is enforced on every path: equipping or using an item, learning a spell from a scroll or a shop, **casting** a spell, and the delevel sweep that strips gear whose requirements a player has fallen below. Unequipping is never blocked — only reaching for something is. **It exists because the stat requirement cannot pace progression on its own.** An item's `Power` doubles as its STR/DEF requirement, but a class's *base* stat is high enough at level 1 that a specialist already meets a mid-ladder piece the day the character is rolled — a Sage meets the level-10 tier's armor immediately. So the stat gate decides **who** may use a thing (it is what stops a Sorcerer in plate) and the level decides **when**; both are checked and the message names whichever failed. Cast is checked as well as learn because a delevel can put a spell you already know out of reach, and unlike gear there is no sweep that unlearns spells — a worn piece keeps applying its stats, whereas a spell only matters at the moment it fires. Currency and keys carry no level: gold is not something you qualify for, and a door refusing its own key because the holder is under-leveled is a puzzle nobody asked for. |
| Time of day | A 4-hour continuous cycle: Day (2 h 30 min), Dusk (15 min), Night (1 h), Dawn (15 min). Night renders as a light map that *multiplies* the world — unlit ground falls to dark navy while every player and light-flagged NPC casts a warm halo with organic per-source flicker, so sprites, names, bars, and floating text always read through the light rather than being hidden by an overlay. Darkness ramps in over Dusk and out over Dawn. Cycle position is saved every 60 s and resumed on restart, so time advances only while the server is online. Nightfall broadcasts a proclamation plus a separate red "NPCs grow stronger in the darkness" warning; night's end broadcasts its own. Joining players see a phase-specific welcome line, and the HUD shows the current phase with a tooltip countdown to the next Night or Day transition. `/tod <day\|dusk\|night\|dawn>` (Developer+) jumps to any phase and announces the forced shift. VB6 had day/night logic but its cycle increment was commented out, so it never ran. |
| NPC light sources | NPCs carry an "Emits Light" flag, editable per NPC type. When set, the NPC casts a night halo whose **color, radius in tiles, flicker style, and intensity** are all configurable, defaulting to the classic warm torch; when unset it emits none. This lets torch-bearing townsfolk or glowing creatures illuminate their surroundings while ordinary monsters stay dark. Defaults off, so existing NPCs are unaffected until opted in. |
| Safe-zone lighting | Safe-zone maps stay lit at night: each emits a steady, non-flickering, map-wide area light covering the whole map and spilling softly past its edges into adjacent wilderness. Towns stay navigable after dark without placing individual lights, and the soft spill marks where the safe area gives way to the dark. |
| Localization | All client, server, and editor UI strings live in per-project `lang/*.json` files rather than hardcoded literals. Placeholders are named (`{Gold:N0}`, `{Damage}`) rather than positional, so translators can reorder words freely, and a validation pass checks translations for missing, extra, or mismatched tokens. Players can **switch language live** from the Options panel without restarting; every panel refreshes its labels automatically. Language files are self-describing — each contains a name key in its own language (e.g. `"Español"`) — so **adding a language is a single JSON file drop**, no manifest or code change. Stub files for English, Spanish, Portuguese, and French are included, and the selected locale persists per client. Server messages localize **per recipient**: a single broadcast — a level-up, a kick notice, an MOTD change, NPC chatter — lands on each client in its own language simultaneously. Pre-session auth responses (account not found, invalid password, ban notices) carry the locale on the request itself, so even login errors arrive in the player's language before a session exists, and the editor's login responses work the same way. Nothing is persisted server-side; logging out discards the session language. VB6 had no localization. |
| Stat label standardization | The four core attribute abbreviations are STR, DEF, INT, SPD across all player-visible text — stats panel, training panel, new-character screen, editor stat columns, and admin commands. "MAGI" becomes "INT" (Intelligence) everywhere VB6 printed it, and "SPEED" becomes "SPD" in every context that used the long form. Critical-hit labels are renamed P-Crit and M-Crit, adopting the P-/M- prefix already used for P-DMG and M-DMG; VB6 used "Crit" and "Spell Crit", which broke the pattern. The same renames are applied internally end-to-end (records, packets, formulas, JSON keys, data files) with no gameplay change. Separately, the equipment concept "Worn" is renamed **Equipped** throughout — the inventory suffix now reads `(Equipped)` — to disambiguate it from the durability sense of "worn" that messages like "Your sword is getting worn." still use. |
| Vital-change chat lines | Heals (AddHp/AddMp/AddSp) and MP/SP drains (SubMp/SubSp) now print chat messages on the same channel HP damage already uses. One unified template covers all three vitals and player, NPC, and self targets: `You restored N hit/mana/stamina points to <Target>.` for the caster, `<Caster> restored N … to you.` for a healed player. Drains read symmetrically: `You drained X for N mana/stamina points.` and `X drained you for N …`. The caster's line is white, the heal target's bright green, the drain victim's bright red. Self-heal reads "to yourself"; an NPC target has no victim-side line, since there's no one to message. Zero-delta cases — already at max for a heal, already at zero for a drain — skip the line, the same way the float number is skipped. Heal-crit flavor is unified too: all three Add crits share one caster line, with a matching target-side line that now fires on MP/SP heal crits as well as HP. VB6 and the earlier port silently moved the bar for non-HP vital changes without saying who did what to whom; HP damage lines are unchanged. |
| `/info` self-default | Typing `/info` with no name targets the requester's own character — the same readout as `/info <ownname>`. VB6 always required an explicit name; an empty argument fell through and reported "Player is not online." |
| Live editor sync | Pushing a map edit refreshes every player who can **see** the map, not only those standing on it: occupants get a full reload, and observers of a neighboring cell get a targeted refresh of tiles, items, NPCs, traversal, and doors. VB6 warped only occupants, leaving anyone on an adjacent map using a stale cached neighbor — a newly added connection wasn't traversable until that player relogged. The client also revalidates revision on a seamless border cross, falling back to a full blocking reload rather than shifting stale tiles into view. Tile data is **replaced** rather than merged, so clearing a wall to default in the editor actually clears it on the server. Saving a map also **re-spawns its tile-defined items immediately**, so a newly placed item tile appears the instant you save; VB6 re-spawned only NPCs, and map items refreshed solely via `/maprespawn` or a 2-minute timer that skipped any map with players on it — including the mapper standing on the map they just edited. The Properties panel shows a read-only Revision counter that bumps on every save, with parity between online push and offline disk write. Separately, a **map group** is an independent client-cached definition, shipped and refreshed like items and NPCs, rather than having its values baked into each member map. A group supplies inheritable defaults — Moral, Music, Shop, Indoors, Always Dark, Boot destination, and display name — that a member map uses wherever its own value is left to inherit, and client and server resolve through the same shared logic so the effective value can't diverge. Editing a group online updates its music, safe-zone status, lighting, and displayed name for every connected player on the next frame — no map reload, no revision bump, no input-block flash. |
| Penalty commands | `/kick` and `/mute` take an optional duration in minutes (default 60, range 1-1440). All three penalties — kick, ban, mute — target an online character by name but apply to that character's **account**, so switching characters or relogging doesn't dodge the timer. Each emits exactly one server-wide broadcast naming target, admin, and duration; kick and ban also show the duration on the disconnect alert and on the subsequent login rejection. A muted player who tries to chat sees a private "you are muted for N more minute(s)" reply on Say, Yell, Broadcast, Tell, Emote, and Roll. Admins are immune to all three, replacing the legacy "target access ≤ your access" gate. The ban subcommands are streamlined: `/banlist` and `/destroybanlist` are gone (admins edit `banlist.json` directly), and `/refreshbanlist` re-reads it after manual edits. Banned accounts can no longer be deleted — the delete flow checks ban status only after the password verifies, so the check never reveals account state to wrong-password probes. `/ban`, `/mute`, and `/refreshbanlist` are also available on the server console alongside `/kick`, with console-issued punishments showing "Console" as the admin name. Account and character creation now reject names containing spaces so the duration parser stays unambiguous; existing names with spaces still load. VB6 had no kick timer, no mute, and no protection against deleting a banned account. |
| Lifting a punishment | VB6 could apply a penalty and had no way to take one back short of hand-editing files. `/unban`, `/unkick` and `/unmute` are **Creator only** — a rung above the commands that issue them, because deciding a punishment was wrong is a different call from making it. They take an **account**, not a character, since nobody kicked or banned is online to be pointed at; an online character's name is accepted and resolved as a convenience. `/unmute` clears the live session's own copy of the timer as well as the account's, or the player would stay muted until they relogged. The same three lifts are on the server console, and both the server window's Moderation tab and the in-game `/moderation` panel list everything in force with the lift beside each row. Both surfaces gather **on request** — the report reads the ban file and sweeps every account — and the server re-pushes after any change, so a lifted row disappears rather than sitting beside a button that would now do nothing. |
| Machine bans (`/hwban`) | An account ban stops an account, not a person: a banned player registers again and walks back in. `/hwban` (Creator, and on the console) bans the **account and the machine together**, so re-registration from that machine is refused at the door as well. The client derives one opaque key from the identifier its OS already keeps for itself — `MachineGuid` on Windows, `/etc/machine-id` on Linux, `IOPlatformUUID` on macOS — hashes it before it leaves the machine, and the server salts it again per-server so a stored key cannot be replayed or compared at another server. Hardware is deliberately **not** enumerated: Linux restricts the DMI serials to root, so a serial-based scheme would leave Linux players materially harder to ban than Windows ones. **Only online players can be `/hwban`ned** — the key lives on the live session and is written down only for people who get banned, so no ordinary player's identifier is ever stored; an offline account is what `/ban` is for. `hardwareBans.mode` decides what a match does: **`Block` (the default)** refuses the login — and the registration — outright, while **`Signal`** lets them in and tells every moderator online instead. Block is the default because `/hwban` is a last resort an operator reaches for deliberately, and nothing is banned until they do; two costs come with it and are worth knowing rather than discovering, in that machines cloned from one disk image share an identifier, and a refusal cannot be explained to the person refused without revealing how the scheme works. `/hwunban <login>` lifts every machine banned under an account, leaving the account ban to `/unban`. VB6 had no equivalent; an IP ban was never implemented despite a comment claiming otherwise. **Operators should note** that a machine identifier is personal data under GDPR-style regimes: the engine minimizes what it keeps — one salted hash, only for people deliberately banned, never for anyone else — but telling players the game does this is the operator's job, not the engine's. |
| Right-click player context menu | Right-clicking a player — on their sprite in the world or their name in the chat log — opens a menu of player-targeted actions filtered by the clicker's access level. Player tier sees `Info`, `Party Invite` (Player and Monitor only — admins can't party), `Whisper`, and `Trade`; Monitor adds `Mute`, `Kick`, and `Ban`; Developer adds `Teleport To` and `Bring Here`; Creator adds a `Set Access ›` submenu with the five tiers. The menu captures every mouse event while open, so clicks never bleed through to chat input, the world, or other panels. `/r` is a no-arg reply shortcut that prefills the chat input with the last whisper partner in either direction, and does nothing silently when there is none. **Player name colors in chat** now use the same rule as overhead world names and the party overlay — Player brown, Monitor green, Mapper cyan, Developer blue, Creator pink, PK bright red (PK overrides access; the aggressor flash is in-world only). Hovering a name underlines it and flips the cursor to a hand. The help panel's command list is filtered per tier, so `/help` lists only commands the viewer can actually run. Two access realignments fell out of this: `/ban` lowered from Mapper+ to Monitor+, `/warpmeto` and `/warptome` raised from Mapper+ to Developer+. The party overlay gains an `×` close glyph opening an inline leave-the-party confirmation. |
| Guard attack grace | Guards warn a non-PK attacker up to three times with `A <Guard> says, 'Watch it!'` (chat and bubble) before aggroing — the fourth swing acquires the target and propagates to nearby comrades exactly as the first swing did in VB6. It applies uniformly to melee and damage spells, so a low-damage caster gets the same three warnings a low-damage melee attacker does. A single hit landing 10% or more of the guard's max HP skips grace entirely and aggros on the first hit. PK-flagged attackers are exempt, since guards already target them on sight. The grace counter is **per-player and independent of the guard's own combat status** — a guard already chasing someone still warns a new attacker from scratch, and that attacker's damage during the grace window is filtered out of aggro re-evaluation, so a bystander can't steal target via raw damage contribution. The counter clears with the same 10-second combat-exit cleanup, so a player who walks away gets a fresh window. AttackWhenAttacked NPCs keep their one-swing-aggro rule; grace is Guard-only. |
| Bank/inventory right-click bulk actions | Right-click slots in the bank panel (Deposit 1/X/All on the inventory column, Withdraw 1/X/All on the bank column) and in the inventory panel (Drop 1/X/All). Non-currency single-slot button actions no longer prompt for confirmation. Buttons and menu items disable when the destination has no room; partial moves proceed with what fits and show a message (e.g. "Only 2 of 5 deposited. The bank is full."). Equipped slots are skipped by bulk Deposit and Drop so the player can't accidentally strip their gear. Currency keeps its existing partial-stack semantics. |
| Multiple tilesets + reworked tile layers | VB6's fixed 4-layer model (`Ground` / `Mask` / `Anim` / `Fringe`, each one index into a single `Tiles.bmp`) is replaced by **two layer types** — **Ground** (drawn below entities) and **Fringe** (above) — each a stack of **5 layers**, where **any** layer can come from **any tileset** and carry a per-layer **Anim** flag. Graphics are organized into per-class folders under `assets/graphics/` (`tiles/`, `sprites/`, `items/`); only `tiles/` is multi-sheet today. Tilesets are auto-discovered at build time: the **leading number** in each filename is its stable sheet index (`0_Tiles.bmp`, `1_dungeon.bmp`, …), so adding or removing a sheet never reshuffles existing maps. The editor adds Layer-Type / Layer-# / **Animated** selectors, a **typeahead Tileset picker**, a **Reload** button that re-scans the asset folders at runtime, **Alt + mouse-wheel** to step the active layer (clamped 1-5, no wrap), a layer-number badge on the cursor, and a per-layer exploded tile preview grouped into Ground / Fringe columns showing each layer's source sheet. **Door reveal** changed accordingly: an open `Key` tile hides the **topmost populated Ground layer** — the door graphic — revealing whatever sits beneath it, and a toolbar **Doors** toggle previews the open state; door passability and line-of-sight are unchanged, so this is purely visual. Each layer stores the tile index (0 = empty), sheet index, and anim flags in one packed value, kept compact in the JSON map files and on the wire. This is the single on-disk format — there is no legacy auto-upgrade; VB6 maps come in through the standalone converter, which emits it directly. |
| Multi-frame tile animation (cycle & pendulum) | The per-layer **Anim** flag is a full frame system. A tile stack's anim-flagged layers are animation **frames** shown one at a time on a shared 250 ms tick: **one** anim layer still blinks on and off, but **two or more** cycle as frames, and each animated stack picks a **style** — **Cycle** (`1→2→3→1`) or **Pendulum** (`1→2→3→2→1`). Ground and Fringe animate independently off the same clock, each reading its own style from its lowest anim layer. Every frame dwells one tick, so an N-frame cycle runs N×250 ms and a pendulum (2N−2)×250 ms. One shared routine drives both the live client and the editor preview, so authored animation matches in-game exactly. **Authoring:** the animated-tile preview defaults on and persists, so mis-flagged tiles are visible at a glance. You flag frames at paint time (the brush's Animated checkbox gains a style dropdown) or edit a **placed** tile — clicking a filled tile in Tile mode opens a per-tile dialog with a checkbox per occupied layer plus one style picker per animated stack, and that click blocks painting and preserves the clipboard. The hover preview marks animated layers with a `*`. VB6 had only a single 2-state blink. |
| Particle weather + combat/spell FX | A pooled, allocation-free particle system renders the five weather states and adds net-new combat visuals. Rain falls as blue streaks that splash where they land; snow flutters down and settles before fading; Heavy Wind drives parallax speed-lines plus tumbling debris. Rain and snow fall straight down in world space, so camera-follow parallax leans them opposite the player's motion and keeps them vertical when moving up or down. Melee attacks throw a crescent blade-arc over the target tile in the attacker's facing direction — sparks fly when the swing connects, while a whiff shows the sparkless arc alone — playing for the attacker and every nearby observer. Spells hurl a typed projectile that homes from caster to live target and bursts on arrival: a colored bullet for drains, a glitter cluster for restores, a gray drop-shadowed cube for give-item. Observers see spell FX for every caster, players and NPCs alike. |
| Blood pools, splatter & trails | Damaging hits leave **blood**. Each hit sprays a short-lived droplet burst and deposits a **persistent stain** on the victim's tile, sized by the **hit's damage as a fraction of the target's max HP** (a concave curve, so even chip hits register) **times a closeness boost that climbs as the victim nears death** — ×1 at full HP up to ×4 on a killing blow, keyed on the HP left *after* the hit. A tile's pool therefore **accelerates** as the target weakens, and the big "death splash" falls out of that accumulation with no special-cased death event; even a one-shot kill splashes big. The blob **grows in rendered size** — from a small spill up to a broad pool 3-4 tiles across — then slowly dissipates, a light stain fading in seconds and a full pool lasting minutes. Around each pool sits a **satellite splatter** of 1-4 px droplets whose count grows with the pool, each **birth-anchored** just past the rim at the size the pool had when it appeared so it never slides as the pool grows. The droplets **fade faster than the pool**, so fresh spatter signals a **recent** wound while a bare pool reads as **old**. Adjacent pools fuse into one seamless mass with an organic lumpy-edged look rather than a grid of discrete circles. A badly wounded entity — at or below **a third of its max HP** — also **drips a trail**, one minimum-size mark on each *fresh* tile it steps to, so it reads as a line of drips rather than a re-darkening of tiles it revisits. **Every entity bleeds on any damage** — players, NPCs, and traversal guests, from PvP, PvE, or NPC-vs-NPC, within a map and across seams. The field is **server-authoritative**, so pools are consistent for all observers and late arrivals see existing pools at their true faded opacity, but only deposit events are broadcast while each client replays the shared decay locally, so a merely-fading pool costs no bandwidth. A **Show Blood** toggle (default on) governs pools, trails, and bursts independently of the floating-numbers toggle. VB6 had no blood or damage decals of any kind. |
| Vital color language | One rule ties spell FX and vital colors together: hue tracks the vital, shape tracks drain vs restore, and green means healing or gain only. Damage-HP is red and heal-HP green; MP is always blue (deep for drain, cyan for restore); SP is amber/gold; give-item is white. So that green can mean "gain" everywhere, SP's identity color moves off green — every SP bar (HUD, stats panel, overhead world bar, party overlay) and the SP potion label recolor from green to amber-yellow. |
| Attribute-driven lighting + heat shader | Light emitters are per-source attributed — color, radius, flicker style, and a stable identity — rather than one hardcoded warm torch, which also fixes a flicker-phase jump when the light list reordered as NPCs spawned or despawned. Glowing spell projectiles push short-lived colored lights into the night light-map plus an additive glow pass that reads through darkness and in daylight. Heat Wave introduces the project's first GPU shader: a heat-haze pixel shader that ripples the world composite with a warm color-grade. |
| Always Lit maps | A third environmental override beside Indoors and Always Dark. An **Always Lit** map stays bright at night: it emits a steady, non-flickering, map-wide area light, and contiguous lit maps tile with no seam while their light spills a little into the wilderness around them. Lit and Dark are mutually exclusive and resolve as a pair, so a map is never both, and both inherit from the map's group. Neither is tied to the map's moral — whether you can be attacked somewhere and whether you can see there are separate claims, where VB6 lit safe zones by virtue of being safe. |
| Blocked tiles choose what they block | A `Blocked` tile stops walkers always, and carries two further flags — **blocks light** and **blocks sight** — that default to **on**, which is what every VB6 wall meant. Turning one off gives an obstacle that stops a body but not a torch or a spell: a railing, a low wall, a pane of glass. Light occlusion and spell line-of-sight each read their own flag, so the two can disagree deliberately. |
| Indoor and Always Dark maps | Two per-map flags govern environmental rendering. **Indoors** suppresses weather particles — rain and snow never fall inside a cave, dungeon, or building — and keeps the map fully lit even at Night, matching a safe-zone town but independent of moral. Crossing indoors by seam or warp instantly clears any weather still in the air, so rain doesn't visibly finish falling once you're under cover; a live weather *state* change still lets particles taper off naturally. **Always Dark** is the inverse: the map reads dark regardless of the time of day, so a dungeon is dark even at noon. Both extend past the map boundary with a 2-tile soft-edge bleed, so a dungeon entrance's dark aura is visible from outside and an indoor cave mouth spills a little light into adjacent wilderness at night. When both flags are set, Always Dark wins for darkness and Indoors still suppresses weather. Always Dark also beats safe-zone lighting — no lit town punch-through — and holds its darkness right to the border against a neighboring lit map. A light-bearer's halo is suppressed on a safe map, since the town light already covers everything, and snaps to full brightness the instant it crosses onto the Always Dark map, keyed to the exact seam. Glowing spell projectiles are the exception, lighting up across the 2-tile fringe instead of snapping at the seam. VB6 defined Indoors on the map record but never wired it to any rendering effect. |
| Editor-placed light sources + night preview | The editor gains a third editing mode — **Light Sources** — beside Tile and Attribute. It hides the tile and attribute palettes and lets you click any tile to place a light, right-click to remove one, and click an existing light to edit it; a dialog sets **color** (a color picker kept two-way in sync with a hex field), **radius in tiles**, **flicker style** (None/Flame/Pulse), and **intensity**, seeded from the classic torch defaults. Placed lights show only in Light Sources mode, as a colored bulb glyph with a faint reach ring, and copy/paste plus undo/redo work exactly like attributes. A **Night Preview** toolbar toggle reproduces the game's night faithfully inside the editor, sharing a single lighting model with the client so the preview and the live game can't drift; toggle it on to view an **Always Dark** map darkened too. At runtime the client emits every placed light through the same warm-halo path as player and NPC torches — at night, and always on Always-Dark maps — culled per-source by its own radius. Unlike players and NPCs it is deliberately **not** town-light-suppressed, so a light authored on a safe-zone map lights up even inside the lit town. Light sources persist in the map JSON and sync online exactly like tiles and NPC slots. |
| Inventory equipment view + sort | Two right-justified links sit above the inventory list. **[Equipment]** opens a paper-doll sub-view of the four equipped pieces (Helmet top-center, then Weapon / Chest / Shield) with each piece's icon, its combat bonus, a hover tooltip, and a right-click **Unequip** action; a footer sums the gear bonus to each derived stat. **Back** returns to the list. **[Sort]** tidies the bag — server-side and persistent — into a fixed order: Gold, then other currencies alphabetically, equipped gear (Weapon/Chest/Helmet/Shield), then unequipped gear by type then strongest bonus then name, keys alphabetically, spell scrolls alphabetically, and Add-then-Sub potions grouped by vital, strongest first — remapping the equipped-slot indices so worn gear stays worn. VB6 had neither an equipment overview nor an inventory sort. |
| Durable saves on economy events | Player state is persisted the moment it changes for actions a player could otherwise undo by hard-disconnecting before the autosave — item drop, pickup, an equipped piece breaking, death (corpse drops, EXP loss, PK timer, gear degrade), and level-up. This closes an item-duplication exploit on drop, where the dropped map item was written immediately while the inventory loss waited for the autosave, and a free-repair / penalty-evasion window on break and death. Shop, bank, and spell-learn are deliberately left alone — both sides of those exchanges share one save file and revert together, so no dupe is possible. Neither the original nor the C# port saved on these events: VB6 persisted on logout, on account or character create/delete, on shutdown, and via an all-online autosave every 10 minutes, leaving a rollback window ten times larger than the port's 60-second autosave. |

## Bug fixes from VB6

- **Spell stamina restore** — The spell type that restores stamina was updating and broadcasting MP instead of SP due to a copy-paste error.
- **Multiple level-ups per kill** — Only one level-up was granted per kill; a player gaining enough EXP to cross multiple levels would lose the excess.
- **NPC item drop chance** — NPCs with a zero drop chance dropped their item on every kill instead of never dropping it.
- **NPC attack cooldown** — The NPC attack cooldown was consumed even when the target was out of range, making NPCs slower to attack than intended.
- **NPC spawn placement** — When searching for a walkable spawn tile, the game placed the NPC at the last valid tile found rather than the first, biasing spawns toward the end of the map.
- **NPC random spawn range** — Random NPC placement silently excluded the rightmost column and bottom row of the map; the sequential fallback scan didn't have this gap, creating an internal contradiction in spawn coverage.
- **Vital clamping** — Potions had no bounds; vitals could exceed their maximum or go negative.
- **Admin access level validation** — The check for an invalid access level was a logical tautology, making the error path unreachable.
- **Equipment durability updates** — The client only received a durability update when an item broke completely; intermediate damage was never communicated, so displayed durability was always stale until breakage.
- **Key item: right-side door trigger** — Key items with a rightward activation direction were checked against the wrong map dimension, causing them to fail silently in the rightmost columns of the map.
- **Key item: consumed on an already-open door** — Using a key while facing an already-unlocked door re-ran the unlock and consumed the key when the door's take flag was set. VB6 guarded its key-trigger tile and movement checks against an open door but left that guard out of the key-item handler. It's now a no-op.
- **Door auto-close shared one timer per map** — VB6 tracked which doors stood open per tile, but kept a single `DoorTimer` per map that *any* door opening re-stamped, and its sweep ran only once that one timer aged out. So opening a second door silently extended the first door's window, on a map with steady door traffic doors could stay open indefinitely, and when the sweep did fire every open door on the map slammed shut in the same instant. Each door now carries its own open timestamp per (tile, layer) and shuts exactly 5 seconds after it opened, independent of every other door.
- **Equip requirement messages** — The message shown when a player didn't meet equipment stat requirements displayed double the actual required value.
- **Spell scroll for known spell** — Using a spell scroll for a spell the player already knew silently destroyed the scroll; it now returns the scroll and shows a message.
- **Spell level requirement and MP cost can go negative** — VB6's formulas `i = Data1 − Int(ClassInt/4)` and `MPReq = i + Data1 + Data2 + Data3` were both unclamped, so a high-Int class learning a low-`Data1` spell saw requirements like "Required Level: −5" and MP costs that could reach zero or below. A non-positive cost trivially passed the mana check and *added* MP on cast, since deducting a negative is a gain. Both values now floor at 1.
- **Inert spell "Level Req" field** — The spell record carried a `LevelReq` byte that was saved to disk, shipped over the wire, and bound to an editor slider — yet nothing ever read it. The learn and cast requirement was *always* derived from the spell's magnitude, so the slider silently did nothing. The dead field was removed end-to-end. **A live level requirement was later added** in its place, on items as well as spells, and is enforced everywhere — see "Level requirements" in the changes table above. The name is the same; nothing else is. The point of this entry is that VB6 shipped a control that did nothing, not that levels are a bad gate.
- **Sprite rendering near the top edge** — Sprites near the top of the map were clipped incorrectly and rendered from the wrong row of the sprite sheet.
- **Walk animation threshold** — The horizontal movement threshold used the vertical tile size constant instead of the horizontal one; harmless because both values were equal.
- **/banlist command** — The `/banlist` admin command was unreachable because its string comparison extracted too few characters, causing it to always fall through to `/ban`.
- **PK flag broadcast** — When a player's PK status changed, the update packet omitted the PK field, so other clients never received the change and name color updates were silently lost.
- **Healing spells in PvP zones** — Beneficial spells (heal, restore MP/SP) sat inside the PvP damage check, so they silently failed and consumed MP when cast on a teammate in a PvP zone. They now bypass the PvP check entirely.
- **Party invitation always rejected** — Sending a party request never recorded the invited player's slot index on either participant. The acceptance handler read a slot index of 0 every time and rejected the join as "not invited." Both sides are now cross-assigned at invitation time.
- **Shop leave message on login** — The warp routine sent the shop's `LeaveSay` based on the player's current map unconditionally. Since joining warps the player to their saved map, logging in onto a shop map fired the leave message before the welcome message for the same shop. The leave call is now gated on actually changing maps.
- **Shop trade with a zero quantity minted free items** — A trade slot's give/get quantities were never validated. A give quantity of 0 passed VB6's `HasItem(...) >= GiveValue` guard unconditionally, since `HasItem >= 0` is always true even when the player holds none of the give item, so the trade executed and handed over the get item for nothing; a get quantity of 0 likewise still delivered a non-currency item. A misconfigured shop therefore dispensed free items. The trade handler now rejects any slot whose give or get side carries a non-positive quantity, the shop-save handler normalizes each side on write, and the shop editor enforces the same rule live.

## Excluded by design

Two VB6 features are deliberately left out. Everything else VB6 defined but never finished — the guild
structures, the bank, weather, the day/night cycle, Indoors, sex-based sprite selection — is implemented
here.

| Feature | Reason omitted |
|---|---|
| System tray icon | Windows-only COM component; no cross-platform equivalent |
| Debug window | Server TX/RX debug logs provide equivalent visibility during development without shipping debug instrumentation in the release build |
